# Integral Psychology

*Consciousness, Spirit, Psychology, Therapy*

**Year:** 2000 · **Format:** Book

Wilber's short textbook of an "integral psychology", condensed from a much longer unpublished work, with correlation charts that line up dozens of other developmental systems against his own.

## At a glance

*Integral Psychology* (*IP*) is a compact outline of how Wilber thinks human development fits together (Note to the Reader). **Waves** are levels of consciousness, from matter to body to mind to soul to spirit. **Streams** are some two dozen developmental lines, such as cognition and morals, that move through the waves at their own pace. **States** include waking, dreaming and peak experiences. The **self** navigates them all (ch. 1–4, 8). The book sets this model in the four quadrants and applies it to therapy, spirituality, cultural evolution and the mind-body problem (ch. 5–15). Read it for Wilber's clearest account of levels and lines; it is "really two books", a short text and long endnotes (Note to the Reader).

## Context

*IP* is a phase-IV book by Wilber's own count. In a long endnote he divides his work into four phases: a Romantic "recaptured-goodness" model, a developmental "growth-to-goodness" model, levels plus lines, and finally the four quadrants. "The present book is, of course, a phase-4 work" (ch. 9, note 15; see [The evolution of Wilber's thought](phases.md)).

The book began, Wilber says, in an old-book shop, where he found that Gustav Fechner, praised in textbooks for making psychology measurable, had also written a book called *Life after Death*. The research that followed became "a very long textbook in two volumes", published first in "a very condensed and edited form" (Note to the Reader). A note gives the textbook's working titles, among them *System, Self, and Structure*, and calls *IP* a condensed version of "the as yet unpublished two-volume work" (Note to the Reader, note 6). The Preface to the revised [*Sex, Ecology, Spirituality*](1995-sex-ecology-spirituality.md) tells the other half of the story: the textbook stalled, *SES* was written instead, and *IP* is "an abridged version" of the textbook (*SES*, Preface).

*IP* builds on *SES*. It takes over the [holon](../concepts/holons.md) (a whole that is also part of a larger whole), the four quadrants and the diagnosis of "flatland", and sends readers to *SES* for the fuller argument (ch. 1, note 3; ch. 6, note 2). [*Integral Spirituality*](2006-integral-spirituality.md) later took the higher states off the top of the developmental ladder and recast the levels "post-metaphysically"; [From SES to Integral Spirituality](../concepts/from-ses-to-integral-spirituality.md) traces those shifts. The wiki quotes Shambhala's ebook of the original edition.

## Summary

**Psychology needs all its schools.** Behaviorism, psychoanalysis, existentialism, cognitive science, and transpersonal and Asian psychologies each took one aspect of consciousness for the whole. An integral psychology tries "to honor and embrace every legitimate aspect of human consciousness" (Part One).

**Waves, streams, states and self.** From the premodern traditions Wilber takes the Great Nest of Being: levels from matter to spirit, each transcending and including the last. He treats these as a space that development moves through, not as fixed rungs (ch. 1). Through the waves run some two dozen relatively independent lines, so a person can be advanced in one and low in another, and overall development "shows no linear or sequential development whatsoever" (ch. 2). Peak experiences of higher states are available at almost any stage, but they are interpreted through the stage of the person having them, and they last only when they become traits: "states into traits" (ch. 1). The self navigates and integrates all of this. Each step of its growth is a "fulcrum" where things can go wrong in a characteristic way (ch. 3, 8).

**All-level, all-quadrant.** Premodernity was "all-level but not all-quadrant": it knew the Great Nest but did not separate art, morals and science. Modernity was "all-quadrant but not all-level": it separated them, then collapsed everything into the exterior, material world Wilber calls **flatland** (ch. 5–6). An integral approach keeps the strengths of both (ch. 6).

**Applications.** Therapy should match the level of the problem, and "awareness in and of itself is curative" (ch. 8). Some meanings of "spirituality" are developmental and some are not (ch. 10). Cultural evolution is real, but every stage brings new pathologies (ch. 12). Postmodernism's lasting truths are constructivism, contextualism and "integral-aperspectivism"; its failure is "aperspectival madness" (ch. 13). The mind-body problem is largely a product of flatland, and it is fully resolved only at nondual stages of development (ch. 14).

## Overview

### Note to the Reader: A Daylight View

Wilber opens with Fechner, who measured the mind but held the "daylight view" that the universe is "inwardly alive and conscious" (Note to the Reader). From Fechner to William James to [James Mark Baldwin](../people/baldwin.md), psychology was "still on speaking terms with the ancient wisdom of the ages", and the book takes that period as its model (Note to the Reader). Its aim is "to help start a discussion, not finish it". Wilber recommends skipping the notes until a second reading (Note to the Reader).

### Part One: Ground (ch. 1–4)

The introduction to Part One lists what psychology studies: the functions, structures, states, modes and development of consciousness. Most schools, Wilber argues, took one of these as the only aspect worth studying. Coordinating them can only proceed with "orienting generalizations", broad claims that most specialists in a field would accept (Part One).

#### 1. The Basic Levels or Waves

The backbone is the perennial philosophy's [Great Chain of Being](../concepts/great-chain-of-being.md), recast as a "Great Nest of Being": matter, body, mind, soul and spirit, each level enveloping the ones below like concentric spheres (ch. 1). Spirit is both the highest level and the ground of all levels (ch. 1). Wilber uses "level", "structure" and "wave" interchangeably, and insists that "There is nothing linear or rigid" about them (ch. 1). The higher levels are "potentials, not absolute givens", "more like Kosmic habits than pregiven molds" (ch. 1). His charts list about sixteen basic structures, grouped into ten functional groupings: sensorimotor, phantasmic-emotional, rep-mind, rule/role mind, formal-reflexive, vision-logic, psychic, subtle, causal and nondual (ch. 1). The middle ones roughly match Jean [Piaget](../people/piaget.md)'s stages: preoperational thinking ("preop"), concrete operational ("conop", rules and roles) and formal operational ("formop", abstract and hypothetical reasoning) (ch. 1).

From Vedanta Wilber takes the difference between **structures** (levels) and **states** (waking, dreaming, deep sleep). Everyone has the states, so a person at almost any stage can have a peak experience of the psychic, subtle, causal or nondual. But the experience is interpreted at that person's stage: a mythic-stage person, for example, may take an experience of God-union as salvation for believers only. That gives a grid of "around sixteen" types of spiritual experience, first set out in *A Sociable God* (ch. 1). Cognition is "necessary (but not sufficient)" for the other lines. Piaget's stages up to formop are, Wilber says, universal, and most researchers find two to four stages beyond formop, which he calls [vision-logic](../concepts/vision-logic.md) (ch. 1). The correlations in the charts are "very general", but most are "accurate to within plus-or-minus 1.5 stages" (ch. 1).

#### 2. The Developmental Lines or Streams

Through the waves flow some two dozen lines: morals, affects, self-identity, cognition, needs, worldviews, several lines that can be called "spiritual", and others ([Waves and Streams](../concepts/waves-and-streams.md); ch. 2). Within a line, stages build on each other and cannot be skipped. Most lines pass through the same broad waves: preconventional, conventional, postconventional and, if higher stages exist, "post-postconventional" (ch. 2). Across lines, though, development is uneven, a fact that "finally undid the Piagetian scheme" (ch. 2). The [integral psychograph](../concepts/integral-psychograph.md) plots one person's lines against the levels (ch. 2). Not all development is hierarchical: "at least half of it" is the non-ranked growth of skills within a level (ch. 2). Wilber acknowledges Howard Gardner as "one of the first" to use the terms "waves" and "streams" (ch. 2, note 1).

#### 3. The Self

The self navigates the waves and streams. Wilber distinguishes the [proximate self](../concepts/proximate-self-and-distal-self.md), experienced as "I", from the distal self, experienced as "me". In development, "the subject of one stage becomes an object of the next" (ch. 3). Each time the self meets a new level it identifies with it, then disidentifies from it, then integrates it from the next level up: a "fulcrum" of self-development (ch. 3). The self's "center of gravity" hovers around one level at a time (ch. 3). The self is also the seat of identity, will, defenses, "metabolism (which converts states into traits)" and integration (ch. 3).

#### 4. The Self-Related Streams

Some lines are tied closely to the self: its identity, morals and needs. When the self's center of gravity is at the late rule/role mind, its sense of self is conformist (Loevinger), its morals are starting to become conventional (Kohlberg), and its main need is belongingness (Maslow) (ch. 4). The chapter surveys theorists of the self, including [Erik Erikson](../people/erikson.md), [Clare Graves](../people/graves.md), [Jane Loevinger](../people/loevinger.md), [Robert Kegan](../people/kegan.md) and [Susanne Cook-Greuter](../people/cook-greuter.md), and of morals, led by [Lawrence Kohlberg](../people/kohlberg.md) and [Carol Gilligan](../people/gilligan.md) (ch. 4). Against the charge that stage theories are sexist or Eurocentric, Wilber quotes textbook reviews finding Kohlberg's stages neither biased against women nor culture-bound (ch. 4).

The longest section presents Don Beck and Christopher Cowan's Spiral Dynamics, built on Graves: eight color-coded value systems from beige (survival) to turquoise (holistic) (ch. 4). The first six are "first-tier": each thinks its own view is the right one. "Second-tier thinking" (yellow and turquoise) sees that every level is needed for the health of the whole spiral (ch. 4). Wilber notes that Spiral Dynamics covers neither states nor transpersonal levels (ch. 4), and a long note adds his reservations and his critique of the pluralist "green" level for resisting second-tier thinking (ch. 4, note 22). The chapter ends with [horizontal types](../concepts/horizontal-types.md) such as the Enneagram, which can occur at any level (ch. 4). The conclusion to Part One sets up Part Two: most modern researchers stop around vision-logic, and modernity's denial of the higher levels is, in Wilber's word, "horrifying" (Conclusion to Part One).

### Part Two: Path (ch. 5–7)

Part Two looks at the transition from premodern to modern worldviews, to take "the enduring insights from both" and drop their limitations (Part Two).

#### 5. What Is Modernity?

Following Max Weber and [Jürgen Habermas](../people/habermas.md), Wilber defines modernity by "the differentiation of the cultural value spheres" of art, morals and science (ch. 5). That differentiation produced modernity's dignities: liberal democracy, the end of slavery, feminism, modern medicine. When it went too far into dissociation, science became scientism and denied the Great Nest altogether: its disaster ([The Dignity and Disaster of Modernity](../concepts/dignity-and-disaster-of-modernity.md); ch. 5). Here Wilber introduces the [four quadrants](../concepts/four-quadrants.md), the interior and exterior of the individual and the collective. He says he noticed that the systems in the charts, "virtually without exception, they fell into four general classes" (ch. 5). The quadrants reduce to the [Big Three](../concepts/big-three.md) of I, we and it: the Beautiful, the Good and the True (ch. 5). A note adds that "there is nothing magical about the number four" (ch. 5, note 2).

#### 6. To Integrate Premodern and Modern

The integral task is to differentiate each level of the Great Nest into the four quadrants (ch. 6). The traditional Chain was all-level, but it put matter on the lowest rung and so missed that brain and mind are related as exterior and interior, not as lower and higher (ch. 6). Modernity at its best was all-quadrant, but it collapsed interiors into exteriors: [flatland](../concepts/flatland.md) (ch. 6). Wilber separates gross reductionism (everything reduced to atoms) from subtle reductionism (every "I" and "we" reduced to systems of "its"), and says the Enlightenment was guilty mainly of the subtle kind. [Systems theory](../concepts/systems-theory.md) "is precisely part of the disease we are trying to overcome" ([The Limits of the Exterior Approach](../concepts/limits-of-the-exterior-approach.md); ch. 6).

#### 7. Some Important Modern Pioneers

Brain states can be mapped as correlates of interior states, but not substituted for them, because exteriors carry no gradations of value: "serotonin is not better than dopamine" (ch. 7). Four pioneers of an integral approach follow. Baldwin, "the most pivotal", traced aesthetic, moral and scientific development through stages up to a "hyperlogical" unity, and Piaget and Kohlberg took up his scheme (ch. 7). Habermas is "all-quadrant, but not quite all-level" (ch. 7). [Sri Aurobindo](../people/aurobindo.md)'s integral yoga joined the Great Nest to evolution, but he did not analyze the cultural and social quadrants (ch. 7). [Abraham Maslow](../people/maslow.md) showed that each level has its own need (ch. 7).

### Part Three: Fruition (ch. 8–15)

Part Three pulls the strands together: the Great Nest from premodernity, the differentiated value spheres from modernity, and their integration, "all-level, all-quadrant" (Part Three).

#### 8. The Archeology of Spirit

The longest chapter turns the model to therapy. Development, simplified, "comes down to waves, streams, and self" (ch. 8). Whether the ego is lost in higher development is, Wilber says, "almost entirely semantic": an exclusive identity with it is lost, but its functions are kept (ch. 8). He lays out nine fulcrums, plus a birth fulcrum, F-0, each with its own pathology and treatment (ch. 8). Failure at F-1 can contribute to psychosis, at F-2 to narcissistic and borderline conditions, and at F-3 to classic neurosis (ch. 8). Neurosis calls for "uncovering" therapy, borderline conditions for "structure building" (ch. 8). At F-4 come "script pathology" and cognitive therapy, at F-5 identity crises, at F-6 existential issues and the centaur (Erikson's term, which Wilber uses for an integrated bodymind self at vision-logic; ch. 4), and from F-7 the pathologies of the soul (ch. 8). Under all the therapies lies one thread: "awareness in and of itself is curative" (ch. 8). Subpersonalities can each sit at a different level (ch. 8). The mythic archetypes of Jungian work are mostly "not transpersonal" (ch. 8; ch. 8, note 25).

The title metaphor treats the higher levels as deeper layers of the self, but an archeology "that unearths the future, not the past" (ch. 8). Because all four quadrants interact, therapy must be cultural, social and political as well as individual: "What does it mean to be a well-adjusted Nazi?" (ch. 8). It closes with a menu of integral practice for each quadrant (ch. 8).

#### 9. Some Important Developmental Streams

Moral span widens from egocentric to ethnocentric to worldcentric to "pneumocentric" (spirit-centered) (ch. 9). Needs form a holarchy of "food": physical, emotional, mental and spiritual (ch. 9). Worldviews run archaic, magic, mythic, rational, existential, and on to the subtle, causal and nondual (ch. 9). The deep features of the basic waves are gender-neutral, though men and women may move through them "in a different voice" (ch. 9). Art can be analyzed by the level producing it and the level it depicts. In that analysis modernity was "a deeper self in a shallower world" (ch. 9).

The chapter's most novel proposal is that gross, subtle and causal cognition may develop alongside each other, not one after another, and likewise three lines of self: ego (frontal), soul (deeper psychic) and Self (Witness) (ch. 9). Wilber calls this a move "from a phase-2 to a phase-3 model" (ch. 9, note 15). The simplest summary of integral psychology, he says, is "waves, streams, and states, ego, soul, and spirit" (ch. 9). Even so, the frontal line, vision-logic and the subtle "cannot be bypassed" (ch. 9; ch. 9, notes 27–28).

#### 10. Spirituality: Stages or Not?

Whether spirituality comes in stages depends on its definition, and Wilber distinguishes five: the highest levels of any line, the sum of those levels, a separate line, an attitude, and peak experiences. Some involve stages and some do not ([Is Spirituality Developmental? The Five Definitions](../concepts/five-definitions-of-spirituality.md); ch. 10). For the separate-line definition he cites Daniel P. Brown and Jack Engler's cross-cultural stages of meditation, John Chirban on Orthodox saints, Highest Yoga Tantra and Evelyn Underhill (ch. 10). Psychological development need not be complete before spiritual development begins (ch. 10). Whatever the definition, authentic spirituality involves practice: "don't just think differently, practice diligently" (ch. 10). New-paradigm and web-of-life thinking change beliefs about the exterior world, not interior consciousness, so they cannot by themselves solve "Gaia's main problem" (ch. 10).

#### 11. Is There a Childhood Spirituality?

"By definitions 1 and 2, no. By definitions 3, 4, and 5, yes. Sort of." (ch. 11). Childhood openness is real but egocentric. What the childhood ego represses is "the preconventional id, not the postconventional God" (ch. 11). The one possibly spiritual aspect of infancy is the "trailing clouds of glory", a deeper psychic awareness that fades as the ego develops (ch. 11). Wilber rejects the Romantic view of infancy as a lost ground, a view "which, incidentally, I once embraced" (ch. 11).

#### 12. Sociocultural Evolution

The traditional Great Chain had four inadequacies: it did not differentiate the quadrants; it did not subdivide the mind, and so confused prerational magic and myth with transrational states (the [pre/trans fallacy](../concepts/pre-trans-fallacy.md)); it did not understand early pathologies; and it lacked evolution, a gap closed by "Plotinus temporalized = evolution" (ch. 12). Evolution so far, he suggests, has unfolded about three-fifths of the Great Nest (ch. 12). He draws on Lenski for social evolution and on [Jean Gebser](../people/gebser.md) and Habermas for cultural evolution (ch. 12).

To answer critics who point to Auschwitz, Wilber offers five tenets: the [dialectic of progress](../concepts/dialectic-of-progress.md), the difference between differentiation and dissociation, between transcendence and repression, between natural and pathological hierarchy ([Dominator vs. Growth Hierarchy](../concepts/dominator-vs-growth-hierarchy.md)), and the hijacking of higher structures by lower impulses (ch. 12). He also separates each era's average mode from its most advanced: the shaman in magic times, the saint in mythic times, the sage in mental times, with the nondual traditions from around 200 CE (ch. 12). The higher stages therefore lie "in our collective future, not our collective past" (ch. 12). Evolution itself is [Spirit-in-action](../concepts/spirit-in-action.md) (ch. 12).

#### 13. From Modernity to Postmodernity

Postmodernity's good news is that interpretation is "an intrinsic feature of the fabric of the universe": exteriors can be seen, but interiors must be interpreted (ch. 13). Its three lasting truths are constructivism, contextualism and integral-aperspectivism, the last a term from Gebser for an outlook that privileges no single perspective (ch. 13). The bad news is that extreme postmodernism denied depth altogether and slid into "aperspectival madness" (ch. 13). Its pluralism comes from a high developmental stance whose existence it denies (ch. 13). Constructive postmodernism weaves the many contexts back together ([Wilber on Postmodernism](../concepts/postmodernism.md); ch. 13).

#### 14. The 1-2-3 of Consciousness Studies

The mind-body problem, Schopenhauer's "world-knot", is largely a product of flatland (ch. 14). Wilber separates four meanings: the felt body and the organism ("Body"), the mind and the brain. Hence "the body is in the mind, but the brain is in the Body" (ch. 14). Physicalism and dualism both fail, he argues, because the full answer is disclosed only at nondual stages (ch. 14). Meanwhile research can proceed in two phases: first "all-quadrant", giving equal weight to first-person, second-person and third-person accounts (the "1-2-3"), then "all-level", adding the stages of development (ch. 14).

#### 15. The Integral Embrace

The last chapter restates what to keep from each era: the Great Nest from premodernity, the differentiation and evolution of the Big Three from modernity, and from postmodernity the view that the Kosmos is "endlessly holonic" (ch. 15). It calls the book "a beginning, not an end", and closes on Spirit-in-action, "now reading it" (ch. 15).

### The charts

The charts, numbered 1a to 11, set the basic structures beside cognitive, self, moral, spiritual and sociocultural stages from other theorists, plus single charts for Habermas and Baldwin (Charts). In the ebook they are page images; the wiki redraws them as tables in the [Chart Index](../charts/index.md), starting from [Chart 1: Wilber Correlations](../charts/chart-1-wilber-correlations.md). The text points to them throughout, for example the basic structures in other systems ([Chart 2](../charts/chart-2-basic-structures-in-other-systems.md); ch. 1), cognitive development ([Chart 3](../charts/chart-3-cognitive-development.md); ch. 1), the self-related stages and Spiral Dynamics ([Chart 4](../charts/chart-4-self-related-stages.md); ch. 4), morals ([Chart 5](../charts/chart-5-morals-and-perspectives.md); ch. 4), spirituality ([Chart 6](../charts/chart-6-stages-of-spirituality.md); ch. 10) and sociocultural evolution ([Chart 9](../charts/chart-9-sociocultural-evolution.md); ch. 12).

### The endnotes

Wilber treats the endnotes as a second book (Note to the Reader). Several important points live there:

- **Wilber's phases.** The four-phase account of his own work (ch. 9, note 15).
- **Structures, states and planes.** Six kinds of structure: levels and lines, enduring and transitional, deep and surface (ch. 1, note 7). Levels of consciousness versus planes of reality, and the three-variable model of structures, states and realms that Wilber dates to *A Sociable God* (ch. 1, note 3; ch. 8, note 2; ch. 12, note 12).
- **Spiral Dynamics and "green".** Reservations about Spiral Dynamics, a critique of pluralistic relativism, and the admission that *SES* was his first polemical book (ch. 4, note 22).
- **Replies to critics.** A "devastating critique" of the centaur (ch. 8, note 8). Allan Combs, whose model Wilber says gets states and structures backward (ch. 12, note 12). Critics who say the average/advanced distinction lets stages be skipped (ch. 12, note 14). Michael Washburn's Romanticism, which Wilber says rests on a "collapsing fallacy" (ch. 9, note 18).
- **Consciousness.** "I am a pan-interiorist": every exterior has an interior, but no single kind of interior, such as feeling, goes all the way down. This is set against Whitehead and David Ray Griffin (ch. 14, note 15).
- **Mechanisms.** The self "metabolizes experience to build structure", which converts states into traits (ch. 10, note 4).

## Key passages

Why peak experiences are not yet development:

> But all of those peak experiences, no matter how profound, are merely temporary, passing, transient states. In order for higher development to occur, those temporary states must become permanent traits. Higher development involves, in part, the conversion of altered states into permanent realizations. In other words, in the upper reaches of evolution, the transpersonal potentials that were only available in temporary states of consciousness are increasingly converted into enduring structures of consciousness (states into traits).
>
> — ch. 1, “The Basic Levels or Waves”

The relative independence of the lines, the claim the whole model rests on:

> These lines are “relatively independent,” which means that, for the most part, they can develop independently of each other, at different rates, with a different dynamic, and on a different time schedule. A person can be very advanced in some lines, medium in others, low in still others—all at the same time. Thus, overall development—the sum total of all these different lines—shows no linear or sequential development whatsoever. (It is that fact which finally undid the Piagetian scheme.)
>
> — ch. 2, “The Developmental Lines or Streams”

The self's "center of gravity", which lets Wilber speak of a person's stage at all:

> Still, empirical evidence has consistently demonstrated that the self’s center of gravity, so to speak, tends to hover around one basic level of consciousness at any given time. This means, for example, that if you give individuals a test of ego development, about 50 percent of their answers will come from one level, and about 25 percent from the level immediately above or below it.
>
> — ch. 3, “The Self”

The book's formula for what premodernity and modernity each got right:

> Thus, the job of an integral psychology (as a subset of integral studies) is to coordinate and integrate the research findings in all of the levels in all of the quadrants. Integral psychology obviously focuses on the Upper-Left quadrant, but the whole point of the integral approach is that for a full understanding of this quadrant, it needs to be seen in the context of all the others. This “all-level, all-quadrant” integration was denied to premodernity (which was all-level but not all-quadrant) and denied to modernity (which was all-quadrant but not all-level).
>
> — ch. 6, “To Integrate Premodern and Modern”

The common thread Wilber finds under every level of therapy:

> Is there a common thread to all these levels of treatment? A common thread to psychoanalytic, cognitive, humanistic, transpersonal? In a very general sense, yes. It is this: awareness in and of itself is curative.
>
> — ch. 8, “The Archeology of Spirit”

The answer to whether spirituality is developmental:

> Those are five of the more common definitions of spirituality. The conclusion: not everything that we can legitimately call “spirituality” shows stage-like development. Nonetheless, many aspects of spirituality turn out, upon closer inspection, to involve one or more aspects that are developmental.
>
> — ch. 10, “Spirituality: Stages or Not?”

How Wilber reconciles cultural evolution with modern atrocity:

> Auschwitz is not the result of rationality. Auschwitz is the result of the many products of rationality being used in irrational ways. Auschwitz is rationality hijacked by tribalism, by an ethnocentric mythology of blood and soil and race, rooted in the land, romantic in its dispositions, barbaric in its ethnic cleansing.
>
> — ch. 12, “Sociocultural Evolution”

The turn from postmodern pluralism to its contradiction:

> Extreme postmodernism thus went from the noble insight that all perspectives need to be given a fair hearing, to the self-contradictory belief that no perspective is better than any other (self-contradictory because their own belief is held to be much better than the alternatives).
>
> — ch. 13, “From Modernity to Postmodernity”

Why Wilber thinks the mind-body problem has resisted philosophers:

> No, the reason that both sides of the argument have generally agreed that the mind-body problem is irresolvable, is not that they aren’t smart enough to figure it out, but that it is only solved in postrational stages of development, stages which are generally suspect, ignored, or actively denied by most rational researchers.
>
> — ch. 14, “The 1-2-3 of Consciousness Studies”

## What's contested

**What's strong.** Waves and streams give a clear way to describe uneven development, and the grid of states and stages explains how a spiritual experience can be real yet distorted by the person interpreting it (ch. 1–2). Matching pathology and treatment to levels is a practical tool (ch. 8).

**What Wilber himself conceded or revised.** He calls the book "merely the briefest outline" (Note to the Reader). His chart correlations are accurate only "to within plus-or-minus 1.5 stages" (ch. 1). He leaves the subtle line's U-shaped development out of a figure because "most theorists contest" it (ch. 9), and calls the evidence for a deeper psychic self in infancy "a modest amount" (ch. 11). He admits that confusion over his "structures" is "due in part to lack of clarity in my exposition" (ch. 1, note 7), and says how far the lines of self really develop independently "only research can tell" (ch. 9, note 20). In *IP* psychic, subtle and causal are still levels above vision-logic; *Integral Spirituality* takes the states off the ladder (see [From SES to Integral Spirituality](../concepts/from-ses-to-integral-spirituality.md)).

**What remains contestable.** Much of the book rests on claimed research consensus: Piaget's stages are "universal and cross-cultural" (ch. 1), and Kohlberg's moral scheme is "largely invariant and universal" (ch. 7). Jeff Meyerhoff's chapter on individual consciousness in [*Bald Ambition*](https://www.integralworld.net/meyerhoff-ba-2.html), which cites *IP* throughout, argues that Wilber "exaggerates the level of agreement in the scientific community". He cites psychologists who reject Piaget's central claims, and notes that Kohlberg dropped stage 6 from scoring for lack of cases. Wilber's own note concedes that "Kohlberg's stage six is an ideal limit, and not an actual stage" (ch. 7, note 12). Other pushback shows in the notes (ch. 8, note 8; ch. 12, notes 12 and 14).

## Concepts in this book

**Levels, lines and the self (Part One)**

- [Waves and Streams](../concepts/waves-and-streams.md): levels and relatively independent lines, the core of the model.
- [The Integral Psychograph](../concepts/integral-psychograph.md): one person's lines plotted against the levels.
- [The Proximate Self and the Distal Self](../concepts/proximate-self-and-distal-self.md): the "I" of one stage becomes the "me" of the next.
- [Horizontal Types](../concepts/horizontal-types.md): typologies such as the Enneagram that cut across all levels.
