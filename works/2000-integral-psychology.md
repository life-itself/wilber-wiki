# Integral Psychology

*Consciousness, Spirit, Psychology, Therapy*

**Year:** 2000 · **Format:** Book

Wilber's short textbook of an "integral psychology", condensed from a much longer unpublished work, with correlation charts that line up dozens of other developmental systems against his own.

## At a glance

*Integral Psychology* (*IP*) is a compact outline of how Wilber thinks human development fits together (Note to the Reader). **Waves** are levels of consciousness, from matter to body to mind to soul to spirit. **Streams** are some two dozen developmental lines, such as cognition and morals, that move through the waves at their own pace. **States** include waking, dreaming and peak experiences. And a **self** has to "navigate, balance, and integrate" all of them (ch. 1–4, 8). The book sets this model in the four quadrants and applies it to therapy, spirituality, cultural evolution and the mind-body problem (ch. 5–15).

Of Wilber's books, this is the one to read for the psychology itself. It is dense and outline-like: "really two books", a short text and long endnotes, which Wilber suggests saving for a second reading (Note to the Reader). For the big picture in conversational form, start with [*A Brief History of Everything*](1996-a-brief-history-of-everything.md); for the full argument about modernity and spirit, read [*Sex, Ecology, Spirituality*](1995-sex-ecology-spirituality.md).

## Context

By Wilber's own count *IP* is a phase-4 work. A long endnote divides his writing into four phases: Romantic, then developmental, then levels plus lines, and finally the four quadrants (ch. 9, note 15; see [The evolution of Wilber's thought](phases.md)).

*IP* condenses a two-volume textbook, *System, Self, and Structure*, that Wilber planned for years and never published. He set it aside to write *SES*, and later brought out *IP* as "an abridged version" of it (Note to the Reader, note 6; *SES*, Preface). That history explains the book's texture: tightly packed chapters and a large apparatus of charts and notes.

*IP* takes over from *SES* the [holon](../concepts/holons.md) (a whole that is also part of a larger whole), the four quadrants and the diagnosis of "flatland", and sends readers there for the fuller argument (ch. 1, note 3; ch. 6, note 2). [*Integral Spirituality*](2006-integral-spirituality.md) later revised the model; [From SES to Integral Spirituality](../concepts/from-ses-to-integral-spirituality.md) traces the shifts.

## Summary

**Psychology needs all its schools.** Behaviorism, psychoanalysis, existentialism, cognitive science, and transpersonal and Asian psychologies each took one aspect of consciousness for the whole. An integral psychology tries "to honor and embrace every legitimate aspect of human consciousness" (Part One).

**Waves, streams, states and self.** From the premodern traditions Wilber takes the Great Nest of Being: levels from matter to spirit, each transcending and including the last. He treats these as a space that development moves through, not as fixed rungs (ch. 1). Through the waves run some two dozen relatively independent lines. Each line unfolds in stages, but a person can be advanced in one and low in another, so overall development "shows no linear or sequential development whatsoever" (ch. 2). Peak experiences of higher states are available at almost any stage, but they are interpreted through the stage of the person having them, and they count as development only when they become lasting traits (ch. 1). The self navigates and integrates all of this, and each step of its growth is a "fulcrum" where things can go wrong in a characteristic way (ch. 3, 8).

**All-level, all-quadrant.** Premodernity was "all-level but not all-quadrant": it knew the Great Nest but did not separate art, morals and science. Modernity was "all-quadrant but not all-level": it separated them, then collapsed everything into the exterior, material world Wilber calls **flatland** (ch. 5–6).

**Applications.** Therapy should match the level of the problem, and beneath every school Wilber finds one common thread, the healing power of awareness itself (ch. 8). Some meanings of "spirituality" are developmental and some are not (ch. 10). Cultural evolution is real, but every stage brings new pathologies (ch. 12). Postmodernism's lasting truths are constructivism, contextualism and "integral-aperspectivism"; its failure is "aperspectival madness" (ch. 13). The mind-body problem is largely a product of flatland, fully resolved only at nondual stages of development (ch. 14).

## Overview

*Under each heading below, claims come from that chapter or section; other locations, such as endnotes, are cited in parentheses.*

### Note to the Reader: A Daylight View

Gustav Fechner, praised in textbooks for making the mind measurable, also wrote *Life after Death* and held the "daylight view" that the universe is "inwardly alive and conscious". From Fechner to William James to [James Mark Baldwin](../people/baldwin.md), psychology was "still on speaking terms with the ancient wisdom of the ages", and the book takes that period as its model.

### Part One: Ground (ch. 1–4)

Part One lays the foundation: the levels, the lines, and the self that moves through them. Most schools of psychology, Wilber argues, took one aspect of consciousness as the only one worth studying. Coordinating them calls for "orienting generalizations", broad claims meant "to simply get us in the right ballpark".

#### 1. The Basic Levels or Waves

The backbone is the perennial philosophy's [Great Chain of Being](../concepts/great-chain-of-being.md), recast as a "Great Nest of Being": matter, body, mind, soul and spirit, each level enveloping the ones below. "There is nothing linear or rigid" about these levels, or waves: the higher ones are "potentials, not absolute givens". The charts list around sixteen, which Wilber condenses into about ten groupings. These run from sensorimotor and emotional, through three stages of mind that roughly match [Jean Piaget](../people/piaget.md)'s (images and symbols, concrete rules and roles, formal abstract reasoning), to [vision-logic](../concepts/vision-logic.md), the integrative thinking researchers find beyond formal reasoning, and then four transpersonal levels: psychic, subtle, causal and nondual.

From Vedanta he takes the difference between **structures**, the levels, and **states**: waking, dreaming, deep sleep. Because everyone has the states, a person at almost any stage can have a peak experience of a higher level, but will interpret it through their own stage. Someone at the mythic stage may take union with God as salvation for believers only.

#### 2. The Developmental Lines or Streams

Through the levels move some two dozen lines, among them morals, affects, self-identity, cognition, needs, worldviews and several that can be called "spiritual" ([Waves and Streams](../concepts/waves-and-streams.md)). Within a line, stages build on each other and cannot be skipped; most lines pass through the same broad waves, preconventional to conventional to postconventional and beyond. Across lines, though, development is uneven, and the [integral psychograph](../concepts/integral-psychograph.md) makes this visible by plotting one person's lines against the levels. Not all development is vertical either: "at least half of it" is the growth of competences within a level.

#### 3. The Self

What holds the waves and streams together is the self. The [proximate self](../concepts/proximate-self-and-distal-self.md) is experienced as "I", the distal self as "me", and in development "the subject of one stage becomes an object of the next". At each new level the self identifies with it, disidentifies from it, then integrates it from the next level up. This passage is a "fulcrum", and the fulcrums later organize the account of therapy (ch. 8). Uneven as the lines are, the self's "center of gravity" tends to hover around one level at a time (see Key passages).

#### 4. The Self-Related Streams

Some lines are tied closely to the self. When its center of gravity is at the late rule/role mind, for example, its sense of self is conformist (Loevinger), its morals are becoming conventional (Kohlberg), and its main need is belongingness (Maslow). The chapter surveys theorists of the self and of morals, from [Jane Loevinger](../people/loevinger.md) and [Robert Kegan](../people/kegan.md) to [Lawrence Kohlberg](../people/kohlberg.md) and [Carol Gilligan](../people/gilligan.md) ([Chart 4](../charts/chart-4-self-related-stages.md), [Chart 5](../charts/chart-5-morals-and-perspectives.md)).

The longest section presents Spiral Dynamics, built on [Clare Graves](../people/graves.md)'s research: eight color-coded value systems from beige (survival) to turquoise (holistic). The first six are "first-tier", each thinking its own view the right one; "second-tier thinking" (yellow and turquoise) sees that every level is needed for the health of the whole spiral. A long note criticizes the pluralist "green" level for resisting second-tier thinking (note 22).

The conclusion to Part One sets the problem for Part Two. As the charts show, most modern researchers stop around vision-logic and ignore or deny the transpersonal levels that premodernity acknowledged, a denial Wilber calls "horrifying".

### Part Two: Path (ch. 5–7)

How did modernity lose most of the levels? The great spiritual systems are a legacy of premodernity, and the Enlightenment defined itself against religion. An integral psychology has to reconcile the two eras, taking "the enduring insights from both" and dropping their limitations.

#### 5. What Is Modernity?

Following Max Weber and [Jürgen Habermas](../people/habermas.md), Wilber defines modernity by "the differentiation of the cultural value spheres" of art, morals and science. That differentiation produced modernity's dignities: liberal democracy, the end of slavery, feminism, modern medicine. Pushed into dissociation, it produced the disaster: science became scientism and denied the Great Nest altogether ([The Dignity and Disaster of Modernity](../concepts/dignity-and-disaster-of-modernity.md)). Here Wilber introduces the [four quadrants](../concepts/four-quadrants.md), the interior and exterior of the individual and the collective, which reduce to the [Big Three](../concepts/big-three.md) of I, we and it.

#### 6. To Integrate Premodern and Modern

The traditional Chain had the levels but put matter on the lowest rung, and so missed that brain and mind are related as exterior and interior, not as lower and higher. Modernity separated the quadrants, then collapsed interiors into exteriors: [flatland](../concepts/flatland.md). Its main fault was not gross reductionism (everything reduced to atoms) but subtle reductionism (every "I" and "we" reduced to systems of "its"). That is why [systems theory](../concepts/systems-theory.md) is no cure: it offers "holistic its instead of atomistic its" ([The Limits of the Exterior Approach](../concepts/limits-of-the-exterior-approach.md)).

#### 7. Some Important Modern Pioneers

Brain states can be mapped as correlates of interior states but not substituted for them, because exterior hierarchies carry no gradations of value (see Key passages). Four pioneers came close to an integral view. Baldwin, "the most pivotal", traced aesthetic, moral and scientific development up to a "hyperlogical" unity. Habermas is "all-quadrant, but not quite all-level". [Sri Aurobindo](../people/aurobindo.md) joined the Great Nest to evolution but left the cultural and social quadrants unanalyzed. [Abraham Maslow](../people/maslow.md) showed that each level has its own need.

### Part Three: Fruition (ch. 8–15)

Part Three joins the Great Nest from premodernity and the differentiated value spheres from modernity, then applies the result to therapy, spirituality, history and the mind-body problem.

#### 8. The Archeology of Spirit

The longest chapter turns the model to therapy. Each fulcrum of the self has a characteristic failure and treatment (there is also a birth fulcrum, F-0):

| Fulcrum | Self emerging | Typical problem | Typical treatment |
|---|---|---|---|
| F-1 | physical self | psychosis | largely resistant; medication |
| F-2 | emotional self | narcissistic and borderline conditions | "structure building" |
| F-3 | early mental self | classic neurosis | "uncovering" therapies |
| F-4 | role self | "script pathology" | cognitive therapy |
| F-5 | self-reflexive ego | identity crisis | Socratic dialogue |
| F-6 | centaur (integrated bodymind) | existential issues | humanistic-existential therapy |
| F-7 to F-9 | soul and spirit | diseases of the soul | transpersonal therapies, contemplative paths |

("Centaur" is Erikson's term, ch. 4.) Beneath all of these Wilber finds one common thread (see Key passages). The mythic archetypes of Jungian work, he argues, are mostly concrete roles of the mythic stage, "not transpersonal", so working with them is usually fulcrum-4 therapy (note 25).

The title treats the higher levels as deeper layers of the self, in an archeology "that unearths the future, not the past". And because the quadrants interact, therapy is also cultural and social: "What does it mean to be a well-adjusted Nazi?"

#### 9. Some Important Developmental Streams

Chapter 9 tests the model on particular lines, then complicates it. Moral span widens from egocentric to ethnocentric to worldcentric to "pneumocentric" (spirit-centered). In art, modernity was "a deeper self in a shallower world": reason, a high level, confined its attention to matter.

The chapter's boldest proposal is that gross, subtle and causal cognition, tied to waking, dreaming and deep sleep, may develop alongside each other rather than in sequence. A note places this in Wilber's own scheme: it moves cognition from a single ladder of levels (his "phase-2" model) to several lines through the levels ("phase-3") (note 15). The self, likewise, may be three lines: ego, soul and Self (or Witness). But parallel lines are no shortcut. For lasting development the ego's frontal line, vision-logic and the subtle "cannot be bypassed".

#### 10. Spirituality: Stages or Not?

Whether spirituality comes in stages depends on the definition. Wilber distinguishes five: the highest levels of any line, the sum of those levels, a separate line, an attitude, and peak experiences; some involve stages and some do not ([Is Spirituality Developmental? The Five Definitions](../concepts/five-definitions-of-spirituality.md); [Chart 6](../charts/chart-6-stages-of-spirituality.md)). Whatever the definition, authentic spirituality involves practice: "don't just think differently, practice diligently". Web-of-life thinking cannot solve the ecological crisis, because it changes beliefs about the exterior world, not interior consciousness; the crisis, Wilber thinks, is at root a shortage of people at the worldcentric stages that move them to care for the global commons.

#### 11. Is There a Childhood Spirituality?

"By definitions 1 and 2, no. By definitions 3, 4, and 5, yes. Sort of." Childhood openness is real but egocentric, and the child's ego mainly represses what lies below it, not above it (see Key passages). The one possibly spiritual aspect of infancy is what Wilber, borrowing from Wordsworth, calls the "trailing clouds of glory": a deeper psychic awareness that may be present early and fades as the ego develops. He rejects the Romantic view of infancy as a lost ground, a view "which, incidentally, I once embraced".

#### 12. Sociocultural Evolution

Chapter 12 turns from the individual to history. The traditional Great Chain did not differentiate the quadrants, did not subdivide the mind (and so confused prerational magic and myth with transrational states, the [pre/trans fallacy](../concepts/pre-trans-fallacy.md)), did not understand early pathologies, and lacked evolution. The last is easily fixed: tilt the Chain on its side and let it unfold in time. "Plotinus temporalized = evolution." Evolution so far has unfolded about three-fifths of the Great Nest, so the higher stages lie "in our collective future, not our collective past". Evolution itself is [Spirit-in-action](../concepts/spirit-in-action.md).

To critics who point to Auschwitz, Wilber answers with five tenets, among them the [dialectic of progress](../concepts/dialectic-of-progress.md), the difference between natural and pathological hierarchy ([Dominator vs. Growth Hierarchy](../concepts/dominator-vs-growth-hierarchy.md)), and the hijacking of higher structures by lower impulses.

#### 13. From Modernity to Postmodernity

Postmodernity's good news is that interpretation is "an intrinsic feature of the fabric of the universe": exteriors can be seen, but interiors must be interpreted. Its lasting truths are constructivism, contextualism and integral-aperspectivism, [Jean Gebser](../people/gebser.md)'s term for an outlook that privileges no single perspective. The bad news is that extreme postmodernism denied depth and slid into "aperspectival madness", its pluralism coming from a high developmental stance whose existence it denies ([Wilber on Postmodernism](../concepts/postmodernism.md)).

#### 14. The 1-2-3 of Consciousness Studies

The mind-body problem, Schopenhauer's "world-knot", is largely a product of flatland. The felt body is inside the mind, because the mind transcends and includes feelings, while the brain is inside the organism: "the body is in the mind, but the brain is in the Body". The two truths look contradictory only in flatland. The full answer is disclosed only at nondual stages, so meanwhile research should first be "all-quadrant", giving equal weight to first-, second- and third-person accounts (the "1-2-3"), then "all-level".

#### 15. The Integral Embrace

The last chapter restates what to keep from each era, adding from postmodernity the view that the Kosmos is "endlessly holonic". It calls the book "a beginning, not an end", and closes on Spirit-in-action, "now reading it".

### The charts

The charts, numbered 1a to 11, set Wilber's basic structures beside cognitive, self, moral, spiritual and sociocultural stages from other theorists. The wiki redraws them as tables in the [Chart Index](../charts/index.md), starting from [Chart 1: Wilber Correlations](../charts/chart-1-wilber-correlations.md).

### The endnotes

The endnotes are where Wilber argues with critics: over a "devastating critique" of the centaur (ch. 8, note 8), with Allan Combs over states and structures (ch. 12, note 12), and with Michael Washburn's Romanticism, which he says rests on a "collapsing fallacy" (ch. 9, note 18). They also set out his "pan-interiorist" view: every exterior has an interior, but no single kind of interior, such as feeling, goes all the way down (ch. 14, note 15).

## Key passages

Why peak experiences are not yet development:

> But all of those peak experiences, no matter how profound, are merely temporary, passing, transient states. In order for higher development to occur, those temporary states must become permanent traits.
>
> — ch. 1, “The Basic Levels or Waves”

The relative independence of the lines, which is what separates this model from a simple ladder:

> A person can be very advanced in some lines, medium in others, low in still others—all at the same time. Thus, overall development—the sum total of all these different lines—shows no linear or sequential development whatsoever. (It is that fact which finally undid the Piagetian scheme.)
>
> — ch. 2, “The Developmental Lines or Streams”

The self's "center of gravity", which lets Wilber speak of a person's stage at all:

> Still, empirical evidence has consistently demonstrated that the self’s center of gravity, so to speak, tends to hover around one basic level of consciousness at any given time.
>
> — ch. 3, “The Self”

Why brain science can map consciousness but not replace it:

> After all, compassion might be morally better than hatred, but serotonin is not better than dopamine; and thus if we reduce consciousness to neurotransmitters, we completely lose all value and meaning.
>
> — ch. 7, “Some Important Modern Pioneers”

The common thread Wilber finds under every level of therapy:

> Is there a common thread to all these levels of treatment? A common thread to psychoanalytic, cognitive, humanistic, transpersonal? In a very general sense, yes. It is this: awareness in and of itself is curative.
>
> — ch. 8, “The Archeology of Spirit”

The pre/trans distinction applied to childhood, against the Romantic view:

> The defenses against id can defend against God, simply because a wall is a wall. But what the childhood ego essentially represses is the preconventional id, not the postconventional God.
>
> — ch. 11, “Is There a Childhood Spirituality?”

How Wilber reconciles cultural evolution with modern atrocity:

> Auschwitz is not the result of rationality. Auschwitz is the result of the many products of rationality being used in irrational ways. Auschwitz is rationality hijacked by tribalism, by an ethnocentric mythology of blood and soil and race...
>
> — ch. 12, “Sociocultural Evolution”

Why Wilber thinks the mind-body problem has resisted philosophers:

> No, the reason that both sides of the argument have generally agreed that the mind-body problem is irresolvable, is not that they aren’t smart enough to figure it out, but that it is only solved in postrational stages of development, stages which are generally suspect, ignored, or actively denied by most rational researchers.
>
> — ch. 14, “The 1-2-3 of Consciousness Studies”

## What's contested

**What's strong.** Waves and streams give a clear way to describe uneven development, and the grid of states and stages explains how a spiritual experience can be real yet distorted by the person interpreting it (ch. 1–2). Matching pathology and treatment to levels is a practical tool (ch. 8). Whatever one makes of the synthesis, the charts are a useful comparative reference, setting dozens of developmental systems side by side.

**What Wilber himself conceded or revised.** He calls the book "merely the briefest outline" (Note to the Reader), and his chart correlations accurate only "to within plus-or-minus 1.5 stages" (ch. 1). He calls the evidence for a deeper psychic self in infancy "a modest amount" (ch. 11). He says how far the lines of self develop independently "only research can tell" (ch. 9, note 20). In *IP* psychic, subtle and causal are still levels above vision-logic; *Integral Spirituality* takes the states off the ladder (see [From SES to Integral Spirituality](../concepts/from-ses-to-integral-spirituality.md)).

**What remains contestable.** The evidence is uneven across the spectrum. For the transpersonal levels, Wilber acknowledges, "orthodox Western psychological research begins to abandon us", and he draws instead on contemplative traditions (ch. 1); his own charts show most modern researchers stopping around vision-logic (Conclusion to Part One). Lower down he claims research consensus: Piaget's stages are "universal and cross-cultural" (ch. 1), and Kohlberg's moral scheme is "largely invariant and universal" (ch. 7). Jeff Meyerhoff's chapter on individual consciousness in [*Bald Ambition*](https://www.integralworld.net/meyerhoff-ba-2.html), which cites *IP* throughout, argues that Wilber "exaggerates the level of agreement in the scientific community", citing psychologists who reject Piaget's central claims and noting that Kohlberg dropped stage 6 from scoring for lack of cases. Wilber's own note concedes that "Kohlberg's stage six is an ideal limit, and not an actual stage" (ch. 7, note 12).

## Concepts in this book

**Levels, lines and the self (Part One)**

- [Waves and Streams](../concepts/waves-and-streams.md): levels and relatively independent lines, the core of the model.
- [The Integral Psychograph](../concepts/integral-psychograph.md): one person's lines plotted against the levels.
- [The Proximate Self and the Distal Self](../concepts/proximate-self-and-distal-self.md): the "I" of one stage becomes the "me" of the next.
- [Horizontal Types](../concepts/horizontal-types.md): typologies such as the Enneagram that cut across all levels.

**Quadrants and flatland (Part Two)**

- [The Four Quadrants](../concepts/four-quadrants.md): introduced here as the four classes the charted systems fall into.
- [The Big Three](../concepts/big-three.md): art, morals and science, whose differentiation defines modernity.
- [Flatland](../concepts/flatland.md): modernity's collapse of every interior into exteriors.

**Spirituality (Part Three)**

- [Is Spirituality Developmental? The Five Definitions](../concepts/five-definitions-of-spirituality.md): ch. 10's answer to the stages question.

- [From SES to Integral Spirituality](../concepts/from-ses-to-integral-spirituality.md): *IP* as the halfway point between *SES* and *Integral Spirituality*.
