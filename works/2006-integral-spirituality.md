# Integral Spirituality

*A Startling New Role for Religion in the Modern and Postmodern World*

**Year:** 2006 · **Format:** Book

Wilber's application of his integral framework to religion and spirituality, and the book in which he sets out his "post-metaphysics".

## At a glance

*Integral Spirituality* (*IS*) applies Wilber's Integral Approach to spirituality and religion, and he calls the result "a manifesto for an Integral Spirituality". It makes three main moves. Spiritual *states*, which meditation trains, are different from developmental *stages*, which meditation cannot see. Spiritual claims must become "post-metaphysical": grounded in perspectives and practices, not in a ready-made ladder of reality. And religion has a new job, helping believers grow from ethnocentric to worldcentric versions of their own faith. It speaks directly to people on a spiritual path, in a deliberately light tone, and opens with a primer for newcomers. The wiki suggests it as the book-length way into Wilber's later thought (see [The evolution of Wilber's thought](phases.md)).

## Context

*IS* belongs to what the wiki calls Wilber's fifth phase, focused on post-metaphysics and perspectives (see [The evolution of Wilber's thought](phases.md)). It builds on the AQAL framework, which Wilber says [*Sex, Ecology, Spirituality*](1995-sex-ecology-spirituality.md) introduced in 1995 (ch. 10, note 1), and on [*Integral Psychology*](2000-integral-psychology.md) (2000), where he says he introduced the idea of developmental altitude as "consciousness per se" (ch. 2). It also corrects earlier Wilber, above all by separating meditative states from developmental stages (ch. 4); [From SES to Integral Spirituality](../concepts/from-ses-to-integral-spirituality.md) traces these shifts. For fuller arguments Wilber points to "Excerpts A–G" from volume 2 of the Kosmos Trilogy, posted on his websites (Note to the Reader). The book's "we" is his colleagues at Integral Institute, whose programmes it often recommends (Note to the Reader; ch. 10).

## Summary

**Integral means more perspectives, not more content.** The Integral Approach maps any field with five elements (quadrants, levels, lines, states and types, together called AQAL). It "does not add any content"; it shows a field what it leaves out. Seen from inside and outside, the four quadrants give eight perspectives, each with its own methods: Integral Methodological Pluralism.

**States are not stages.** States of consciousness (waking, dreaming, deep sleep and their meditative versions) come and go at almost any stage of development. Stages are lasting, cannot be skipped, and are invisible to meditation; only outside, third-person research finds them. In the Wilber-Combs Lattice, anyone can have a gross, subtle, causal or nondual state experience, but will interpret it through their stage. Enlightenment becomes oneness with all the states and all the stages that exist at a given time.

**The traditions mastered one quadrant.** The contemplative traditions are experts in the individual interior, especially trained states, but cannot see developmental stages or the psychological shadow. Wilber's advice to practitioners is "Supplement!": keep your path and add what it misses, for example through Integral Life Practice, which pairs meditation with framework study, shadow work and bodily practice.

**Spirit has three faces.** Spirit can be met in the first person, as the great I of one's own awareness; in the second person, as the great Thou of devotion; and in the third person, as the great It or Web of Life. Wilber argues that "new paradigm" spirituality keeps the first and third and has lost the second.

**Spirituality must become post-metaphysical.** The traditions' metaphysics survived neither modernity's demand for evidence nor postmodernity's insight that all knowing is shaped by culture. Wilber treats spiritual levels as evolved "Kosmic habits", not pre-existing planes of reality, and holds that a claim about Spirit means something only when it comes with the practice that would let you look for yourself. On this view the old problem of proving God's existence is not solved but dissolved.

**Religion as conveyor belt.** By Wilber's estimate between half and 70% of the world's population is at ethnocentric stages or below, and religion owns the myths of those stages. So religion alone can legitimate worldcentric and higher versions of its own message, carrying people up the stages, and it should teach contemplative states, which he says speed that growth. This is the "startling new role" of the subtitle.

## Overview

*Each subsection covers the chapters in its heading; a citation is given only where a point comes from elsewhere in the book.*

The book has two spines: the distinction between states and stages (ch. 2–4), which reorganises how spiritual development is understood, and post-metaphysics (ch. 1 and Appendix II), which changes what a spiritual claim can mean; chapter 9 draws the practical consequence for religion.

### A Note to the Reader and Introduction

The Introduction is a primer that readers who know the Integral Approach can skip (Note to the Reader). Stages run from egocentric ("me") to ethnocentric ("us") to worldcentric ("all of us"); uneven growth across lines gives each person an [integral psychograph](../concepts/integral-psychograph.md); [types](../concepts/horizontal-types.md) include masculine and feminine; and the [four quadrants](../concepts/four-quadrants.md) match the pronouns I, we, it and its, and the [Big Three](../concepts/big-three.md) of art, morals and science.

### 1. Integral Methodological Pluralism

If modern and postmodern thought have "trashed" the traditions' metaphysics, as the chapter's first sentence says, what could replace it? Wilber starts with method. Any [holon](../concepts/holons.md), a whole that is also part of a larger whole, can be viewed from its own inside or outside in each quadrant, giving "8 primordial perspectives". Each comes with its own methods, and the book refers to the resulting zones by number:

| Zone | Quadrant | View | Example methods |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Upper Left (I) | inside | phenomenology, introspection, meditation |
| 2 | Upper Left (I) | outside | structuralism, developmental stage research |
| 3 | Lower Left (we) | inside | hermeneutics |
| 4 | Lower Left (we) | outside | ethnomethodology, semiotics, genealogy (ch. 7) |
| 5 | Upper Right (it) | inside | autopoiesis, cognitive science (ch. 8) |
| 6 | Upper Right (it) | outside | behaviourism, empiricism, brain research (ch. 8) |
| 7 | Lower Right (its) | inside | social autopoiesis (Luhmann) (ch. 8) |
| 8 | Lower Right (its) | outside | systems theory (ch. 8) |

Meditation (zone 1) and Spiral Dynamics, a stage model of values (zone 2), are each blind to what the other sees (see the first Key passage).

The chapter then begins the case that Appendix II completes. Integral post-metaphysics "replaces *perceptions* with *perspectives*": nothing is simply given to awareness, because everything is seen from some perspective. The traditions could not answer postmodernity's insight that "*all perspectives are embedded in bodies and in cultures*", yet "*virtually the entire Great Chain fits into the Upper-Left quadrant*" ([The Great Chain of Being](../concepts/great-chain-of-being.md)), the one area they mastered. Their weakness is what [Habermas](../people/habermas.md) calls "the philosophy of consciousness": meditation is "monological", blind to how culture shapes what the meditator sees. So they suffered a "double death": modernity ignored subjectivity, and they ignored intersubjectivity. Integral Methodological Pluralism keeps their truths and drops only "their claim to absoluteness" (see [Wilber on Postmodernism](../concepts/postmodernism.md)).

### 2–3. Stages and States of Consciousness

Chapter 2 views the individual interior from outside (zone 2), in a research tradition Wilber traces to James Mark [Baldwin](../people/baldwin.md). Carol [Gilligan](../people/gilligan.md)'s three stages of women's moral reasoning (egocentric, ethnocentric and worldcentric, in Wilber's terms) show how the first and last can look alike; confusing them is the [pre/trans fallacy](../concepts/pre-trans-fallacy.md), which "applies only to stages, not to states". Meditation cannot see them: "*There are at present no major schools of meditation, contemplation, or prayer that take these types of stages into account*".

There are "at least **a dozen different developmental lines**" ([Waves and Streams](../concepts/waves-and-streams.md)). What makes a level "higher" across such different lines? Wilber uses two answers: cognitive growth is "necessary but not sufficient" for growth in the others, and altitude is "consciousness per se", a degree of consciousness common to every line. He labels altitude with rainbow colours, after the chakra system: as the book uses them, magenta is magic, red egocentric power, amber mythic and ethnocentric, orange rational and worldcentric, green pluralistic, teal and turquoise the newest levels (turquoise being integral), and indigo, violet and ultraviolet lie above (ch. 5; ch. 9; Appendix II).

Chapter 3 is the view from inside (zone 1), where one experiences states, never structures. Vedanta's natural states (waking, dreaming, deep sleep, witnessing and nondual) are available "to all humans at virtually any stage of growth" ([The Psychic, Subtle, Causal, and Nondual](../concepts/psychic-subtle-causal-nondual.md)). Trained in meditation, they unfold as **state-stages** from gross to subtle to causal to nondual, which, unlike **structure-stages**, can be peak-experienced ahead of time.

### 4. States and Stages

How do the two kinds of stage relate, say the value levels of Spiral Dynamics, based on Clare [Graves](../people/graves.md)'s research, and Zen's Ten Oxherding Pictures? Wilber says researchers, himself included, long stacked the meditative stages on top of the highest Western stages. But then no one 2,000 years ago, not even the Buddha, could have been enlightened, since some of those stages are recent. The problem "sat stalled for about two decades".

The answer was the **Wilber-Combs Lattice**, named for Wilber and Allan Combs, who reached a similar idea independently. It puts [Gebser](../people/gebser.md)'s structure-stages up one side and the states across the top: a subtle-state experience of Christ will be interpreted magically, mythically, rationally or pluralistically, depending on the stage of the person who has it. Evelyn Underhill's stages of the mystic path are state-stages and James Fowler's stages of faith, the line Wilber calls "spiritual intelligence" (ch. 2), are structure-stages, so they fit the lattice's two axes. One problem remains: if the world of form keeps evolving, can anyone be fully enlightened? The answer comes in Appendix II. The chapter closes with four meanings of "spiritual" ([Is Spirituality Developmental?](../concepts/five-definitions-of-spirituality.md)).

### 5–6. Boomeritis Buddhism and the Shadow

If meditation cannot see stages, what happens when a generation of meditators interprets its experiences from one stage? Chapter 5's case is American Buddhism. "Boomeritis" is pluralistic green infected with egocentric red: openness that lets narcissistic impulses relabel themselves as high and even spiritual. So "the religion of no-ego" often became "the religion of express your ego", and "*none of this can be seen with the tools of Buddhism*". Wilber advises practitioners not to change their practice but to supplement it with stage models, the eight zones and Integral Life Practice.

Chapter 6 turns to the shadow, the disowned parts of the self, where meditation does worse than miss the problem. Anger I deny becomes a "you" I project, then an "it", a symptom such as depression. Following Robert [Kegan](../people/kegan.md), growth makes "the subject of one stage" into "the object of the subject of the next": healthy development turns "I" into "me", pathology turns "I" into "it" ([Repression](../concepts/repression.md)). Meditation teaches letting go, but letting go of anger not yet owned deepens the split. Integral Institute's 3-2-1 Process turns the "it" back into a "you" and then an "I", so it can be owned before it is let go.

### 7–8. The We and the Right-Hand World

Chapters 7 and 8 turn to the other quadrants. Chapter 7 calls Web-of-Life lists that stack societies on top of organisms "deeply, deeply, deeply confused" ([Systems Theory](../concepts/systems-theory.md)); drawing on [Whitehead](../people/whitehead.md) and Niklas Luhmann, Wilber argues that "the we is not a super-I": an individual has a "dominant monad", a group only a "dominant mode of discourse", which can jump stages as members come and go. The chapter then sets out the three faces of Spirit, arguing that without a second-person Thou the ego can hide in first- and third-person spirituality.

Chapter 8 makes three points. A meditative state's brain-state correlate says nothing about whether what the meditator perceives is real; to find out, "*get into the stage or state from which the sentence is being written, and then look for yourself*". Systems theory is one valid zone that becomes "subtle reductionism and monological imperialism" when it claims the whole picture. And most spiritual writing still assumes the "myth of the given", the belief that reality is simply given to consciousness.

### 9. The Conveyor Belt

Wilber calls this "the single greatest problem facing the world in the interior quadrants". By his estimate 50–70% of the world's population is ethnocentric or lower, and the great religions own the beliefs of those stages. In research for a forthcoming book on terrorism, he says, the attackers all had "amber beliefs with red self". Modern culture puts an "orange pressure-cooker lid" on mythic religion, leaving believers no way to grow from a mythic to a rational faith.

The root is the **Level/Line Fallacy**, "*the confusing of a level in a line with the line itself*". In rejecting the mythic God, the Enlightenment threw out the whole spiritual line ([The Death of God](../concepts/death-of-god.md)). This adds a cause to the [dignity and disaster of modernity](../concepts/dignity-and-disaster-of-modernity.md): with no "Big 4" that included spirituality, science took over its ultimate concerns as scientism.

The cure is religion as a **conveyor belt**. Only religions, which hold the myths, can sanction rational and higher versions of their own message, while respecting each stage as a "station" in life where people may stop. They should also make contemplative states central, since, Wilber says, research shows meditation speeds development.

### 10. Integral Life Practice

Integral Life Practice (ILP) is the theory's practical side: four core modules (the AQAL framework, meditation, the 3-2-1 shadow process and a "3-Body Workout") plus auxiliary ones such as ethics and relationships, with one practice from each done together. Wilber claims ILP is the first practice based on all eight zones; [Aurobindo](../people/aurobindo.md)'s Integral Yoga, by his count, recognised four. The chapter ends with AQAL as "a meta-theoria derived from a meta-praxis", a map to be dropped once Spirit is recognised.

### Appendixes

#### I. From the Great Chain of Being to Postmodernism in 3 Easy Steps

The traditional Great Chain runs matter, body, mind, soul, spirit, with Spirit first stepping down into matter by [involution](../concepts/involution-and-evolution.md) and then evolving back. Wilber keeps the experiences but revises the "*interpretations*" in three steps. Matter is not the bottom rung but "the *exterior form* of *every* rung" ([Interiority](../concepts/interiority.md)); the cultural and social quadrants are added; and subtle energies are placed in the Upper-Right quadrant as hypotheses.

#### II. Integral Post-Metaphysics

Following Kant, Wilber defines metaphysics as thinking "that falls prey to the myth of the given". The traditions' levels "are not pre-existing structures lying around out there"; any surviving version must meet modernity's demand "for objective evidence" and postmodernity's demand "for intersubjective grounding".

Borrowing Charles Peirce's idea of natural laws as habits, Wilber calls levels **Kosmic habits**: once enough people respond at a new level, the response settles into a structure that every later human must develop through. This answers chapter 4's remaining problem. Enlightenment is "**the realization of oneness with all the major states and major structures that are in existence at any given time in history**", so the Buddha was fully enlightened in his time, though the same attainment would not be full enlightenment today.

Every referent has a **Kosmic address**, "altitude + perspective", for both perceiver and perceived: ecosystems are disclosed only from turquoise altitude, and "*There simply is no such thing as “the dog”*", only different dogs enacted at different altitudes. And "the meaning of a statement is the injunction of its enactment" ([Paradigm as Injunction](../concepts/paradigm-as-injunction.md)), so problems such as proving God's existence do not "arise in the first place".

#### III. The Myth of the Given Lives On

Appendix III argues that the humanities, not science, rejected spirituality. In a "Great Left-Hand War", the Intersubjectivists ([Foucault](../people/foucault.md), [Derrida](../people/derrida.md) and others) defeated the Subjectivists (Husserl's phenomenology, existentialism), dismissing introspection as caught in the myth of the given. Wilber then reviews more than a dozen books and a film, from Capra to Thich Nhat Hanh, crediting each but faulting its failure to absorb the postmodern turn.

## Key passages

Why meditation and developmental research need each other, the book's starting point:

> Here’s the point: you can sit on your meditation mat for decades, and you will NEVER see anything resembling the stages of Spiral Dynamics. And you can study Spiral Dynamics till the cows come home, and you will NEVER have a *satori*. And the integral point is, if you don’t include both, you will likely never understand human beings or their relation to Reality, divine or otherwise.
>
> — ch. 1, “Integral Methodological Pluralism”

The core of the Wilber-Combs Lattice: any state, interpreted from any stage:

> The point is that a person can have a profound peak, religious, spiritual, or meditative experience of, say, a subtle light or causal emptiness, but they will interpret that experience with the only equipment they have, namely, the tools of the stage of development they are at. A person at magic will interpret them magically, a person at mythic will interpret them mythically, a person at pluralistic will interpret them pluralistically, and so on.
>
> — ch. 4, “States and Stages”

Why meditation alone does not heal the shadow:

> This can be summarized very succinctly: dis-identifying with an owned self is transcendence; dis-identifying with a disowned self is double dissociation.
>
> Meditation does both.
>
> — ch. 6, “The Shadow and the Disowned Self”

What Wilber thinks contemporary spirituality has lost:

> In today’s “new paradigm” spiritual movements, we usually see the opposite problem: *a complete loss of Spirit in 2nd-person*. What we find instead are extensive descriptions of Spirit in its 3rd-person mode, such as Gaia, the Web of Life, systems theory, akashic fields, chaos theory, and so on. This is coupled, to the extent there is a practice, with Spirit in 1st-person modes: meditation, contemplation, Big Mind, Big Self, Big Me. But no conceptions of a Great Thou, to whom surrender and devotion is the only response.
>
> — ch. 7, “A Miracle Called ‘We’”

The "startling new role" of the subtitle:

> ...precisely because of its ownership of the pre-rational heritage of humanity (and the pre-rational corpus of the great myths), religion alone can help its followers move from the pre-rational, mythic-membership, ethnocentric, absolutistic version of its message to the rational-perspectival, worldcentric, postconventional versions of its own message. *This jump from ethnocentric amber to worldcentric orange is the great leap that religions alone can help humanity make*.
>
> — ch. 9, “The Conveyor Belt”

The first step from metaphysics to post-metaphysics:

> Thus, what the premodern sages took to be META-physical realities are in many cases INTRA-physical realities: they are not above matter, nor beyond nature, nor meta-physical, nor super-natural: they are not above nature but within nature, not beyond matter but interior to it.
>
> — Appendix I, “From the Great Chain of Being to Postmodernism in 3 Easy Steps”

Wilber's test for whether a spiritual claim means anything:

> The meaning of any assertion is therefore, among other things, the injunctions or means or exemplars for enacting the worldspace in which the referent exists or is said to exist... Hence, in shorthand, **the meaning of a statement is the injunction of its enactment**. No injunction, no enactment, no meaning. That is, mere metaphysics.
>
> — Appendix II, “Integral Post-Metaphysics”

## What's contested

This section covers tensions within the book itself, not the wider critical literature.

**What's strong.** The state/stage distinction gives a simple explanation of a familiar puzzle: why skilled meditators can hold conformist or ethnocentric views (ch. 4). The shadow chapter names a real gap between contemplative practice and psychotherapy (ch. 6). The Level/Line Fallacy offers a clear account of how modernity discarded spirituality wholesale while objecting only to its mythic form (ch. 9).

**Does post-metaphysics escape metaphysics?** This is the question the book most invites. Wilber's levels are "Kosmic habits", not eternal planes, but the evolution that lays the habits down still needs a driver, and for that he keeps a "minimalist Eros" as an "involutionary given" (Appendix II, note 3). He concedes that no philosophy can do entirely without *a priori* forms, only "the fewer, the better", and calls the whole account "the most general of orientating generalizations", with "most of the detailed work" still to do (Appendix II). Readers can judge whether what remains is a minimal metaphysics rather than none.

**Numbers and sourcing.** The population shares at each level are "a composite result of several sources" (Appendix II, note 6). The claim that meditation moves people about two stages in four years is "just a very general finding" (ch. 6). The terrorist profile comes from Wilber's own research for a forthcoming book (ch. 9).

**Style and scope.** Putting it "in the bluntest terms possible", Wilber says around 70% of the world's population "is Nazis"; he explains that this is "simply an extreme way to state" that most people have not yet stably reached worldcentric levels (ch. 9). Appendix III judges more than a dozen books in a paragraph or so each. Don Beck, co-developer of Spiral Dynamics, "strongly disagrees" with Wilber's view that it is not a complete model (ch. 4, note 2). Wilber also admits his earlier error of stacking states on top of stages, including in *Up from Eden* (ch. 4; Appendix II, note 4). On what is less new than it looks, see [From SES to Integral Spirituality](../concepts/from-ses-to-integral-spirituality.md).
