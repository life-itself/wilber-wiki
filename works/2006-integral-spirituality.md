# Integral Spirituality

*A Startling New Role for Religion in the Modern and Postmodern World*

**Year:** 2006 · **Format:** Book

Wilber's application of his integral framework to religion and spirituality, and the book in which he sets out his "post-metaphysics".

## At a glance

*Integral Spirituality* (*IS*) applies what Wilber calls the Integral Approach to spirituality (Note to the Reader). A 32-page Introduction explains the approach first. The book then takes up "perhaps 4 or 5" pressing issues, among them proof of Spirit's existence, stages of spiritual development and the role of meditation, and calls the result "a manifesto for an Integral Spirituality" (Note to the Reader). Its main tools are eight methodological "zones", a distinction between states and stages of consciousness, and a "post-metaphysical" account of spiritual levels (ch. 1; ch. 4; Appendix II). Read it for Wilber's later framework. He chose a deliberately light tone, "toward lightness and even frivolity", for a serious subject (Note to the Reader).

## Context

*IS* belongs to what the wiki calls Wilber's fifth phase, post-metaphysics and perspectives, and is where the wiki suggests starting on it (see [The evolution of Wilber's thought](phases.md)). It builds on the AQAL framework, which Wilber says [*Sex, Ecology, Spirituality*](1995-sex-ecology-spirituality.md) introduced in 1995 (ch. 10, note 1). It also draws on [*Integral Psychology*](2000-integral-psychology.md), where he says one of his accounts of developmental "altitude" was introduced and the meanings of "spiritual" are "pursued at length" (ch. 2; ch. 4). He dates the explicit formulation of the eight perspectives and methodologies to 2000 (ch. 10, note 1). Throughout, he refers readers to "Excerpts A–G" for fuller treatments: excerpts from volume 2 of the Kosmos Trilogy, posted on his websites (Note to the Reader; Appendix I).

The book also revises earlier Wilber. He says that he and other researchers stacked meditative states on top of the Western developmental stages, and that the problem "sat stalled for about two decades" (ch. 4). He names his own *Up from Eden* as an example (Appendix II, note 4). The words psychic, subtle and causal are now kept for bodies and states, not for stages (ch. 2, note 4). [From SES to Integral Spirituality](../concepts/from-ses-to-integral-spirituality.md) traces these shifts across the three books. Wilber writes "we" for his colleagues at Integral Institute, and the book often points readers to its programmes (Note to the Reader; ch. 10).

This wiki quotes Shambhala's ebook (Integral Books imprint) of the 2006 book (title page; copyright page).

## Summary

**Integral means more perspectives, not more content.** The Integral Approach maps any field with five elements: quadrants, levels, lines, states and types, together called AQAL (Introduction). It "does not add any content"; it shows a field what it leaves out (Note to the Reader). Each quadrant can be looked at from inside or outside, which gives eight perspectives, each with its own family of methods. Wilber calls the set Integral Methodological Pluralism (ch. 1).

**States are not stages.** States of consciousness (waking, dreaming, deep sleep and their meditative versions) come and go, and are available at almost any stage of development. Stages are lasting, cannot be skipped, and cannot be seen by meditation: only outside, third-person research finds them (Introduction; ch. 2–3). The Wilber-Combs Lattice crosses the two. Anyone can have a gross, subtle, causal or nondual state experience, but will interpret it through their stage (ch. 4). Enlightenment becomes oneness with all the states and all the stages that exist at a given time (ch. 4; Appendix II).

**The traditions mastered one quadrant.** The contemplative traditions are experts in the individual interior, especially trained states, and the Great Chain of Being fits almost entirely there (ch. 1). But they cannot see developmental stages or the psychological shadow. Wilber's advice to practitioners is "supplement": keep your path and add what it misses (ch. 5–6).

**Spirituality must become post-metaphysical.** Modernity demanded evidence, and postmodernity showed that all knowing is shaped by culture; the traditions' metaphysics survived neither (ch. 1; Appendix III). Wilber's alternative treats spiritual levels as evolved "Kosmic habits" rather than pre-existing planes of reality. To claim that anything exists, you must give its "Kosmic address" and the practice that discloses it (Appendix II).

**Religion as conveyor belt.** By Wilber's estimate between half and 70% of the world's population is at ethnocentric stages or below, and religion owns the myths of those stages (ch. 9). So religion alone can legitimate worldcentric and higher versions of its own message, carrying people up the stages. It should also teach contemplative states, which speed that growth (ch. 9). This is the "startling new role" of the subtitle (ch. 9).

## Overview

### A Note to the Reader and Introduction

Wilber explains that the Introduction is a primer on the Integral Approach, which readers who know it can skip (Note to the Reader). The Introduction distils an "Integral Map", also called the Integral Operating System (IOS), into the five elements (Introduction). **States** are temporary, like waking, dreaming and deep sleep. **Stages** or levels are lasting milestones, summarised as a move from egocentric ("me") to ethnocentric ("us") to worldcentric ("all of us") (Introduction). **Lines** are the capacities that develop unevenly, such as cognitive, emotional or moral growth; a person's profile across them is the [integral psychograph](../concepts/integral-psychograph.md) (Introduction). **Types**, such as masculine and feminine, can appear at any stage or state ([horizontal types](../concepts/horizontal-types.md); Introduction). The [four quadrants](../concepts/four-quadrants.md) are the inside and outside of the individual and the collective, matching the pronouns I, we, it and its, and the [Big Three](../concepts/big-three.md) of art, morals and science (Introduction). Wilber adds that "you cannot skip actual *stages*", but state practices such as meditation can speed growth through them (Introduction). The Introduction ends with applications to medicine, business, ecology and practice (Introduction).

### 1. Integral Methodological Pluralism

The chapter opens with the claim that the metaphysics of the spiritual traditions has been "trashed" by modern and postmodern thought (ch. 1). Any [holon](../concepts/holons.md), any whole that is also part of a larger whole, can be viewed from its own inside or outside in each quadrant, giving "8 primordial perspectives" (ch. 1). Each perspective goes with methods: phenomenology and structuralism for the individual interior, hermeneutics and ethnomethodology for the collective interior, and so on round the quadrants (ch. 1). The everyday example is meditation and Spiral Dynamics, a stage model of values: you can meditate for decades and never see a Spiral Dynamics stage, or study the stages and never have a satori (ch. 1). Each zone, with its practices and the world it discloses, is a "hori-zone" (ch. 1). A short section on "integral math" writes these as perspectives on perspectives, so meditation is "1-p × 1-p × 1p" (ch. 1).

The section on Integral Post-Metaphysics carries the book's main argument. Integral post-metaphysics "replaces *perceptions* with *perspectives*" (ch. 1). The traditions failed modernity's demand for evidence and postmodernity's insight that "*all perspectives are embedded in bodies and in cultures*" (ch. 1). Yet they specialised in the individual interior, and "*virtually the entire Great Chain fits into the Upper-Left quadrant*" ([The Great Chain of Being](../concepts/great-chain-of-being.md); ch. 1). Following [Habermas](../people/habermas.md), Wilber calls their stance "the philosophy of consciousness": meditation is "monological", blind to how culture shapes what the meditator sees (ch. 1). The traditions thus suffered a "double death", from modernity's taboo of subjectivity and their own taboo of intersubjectivity (ch. 1). Integral Methodological Pluralism keeps all the truths and drops only "their claim to absoluteness" (ch. 1). See [Wilber on Postmodernism](../concepts/postmodernism.md).

### 2–3. Stages and States of Consciousness

Chapter 2 is about zone #2, the scientific study of the interior from outside. Carol [Gilligan](../people/gilligan.md)'s study of women's moral reasoning found three stages, which Wilber calls egocentric, ethnocentric and worldcentric (ch. 2). Because the first and last can give the same answer, they are easily confused: this is the [pre/trans fallacy](../concepts/pre-trans-fallacy.md), which Wilber says "applies only to stages, not to states" (ch. 2). He credits James Mark [Baldwin](../people/baldwin.md) with the first successful form of developmental structuralism, and says these stages are invisible to meditation: "*There are at present no major schools of meditation, contemplation, or prayer that take these types of stages into account*" (ch. 2).

There are "at least **a dozen different developmental lines**", each answering one of "the questions that life itself poses", such as "What am I aware of?" (cognitive) or "What is of ultimate concern?" (spiritual) (ch. 2). The lines are also called streams, and their levels waves ([Waves and Streams](../concepts/waves-and-streams.md); ch. 2). Two theories explain what makes a level "higher" across lines, and AQAL uses both: the cognitive line is "necessary but not sufficient" for the others, and altitude is "consciousness per se" (ch. 2). He labels altitude with rainbow colours (red, amber, orange, green, teal, turquoise, indigo and so on), borrowed from the chakra system (ch. 2). The idea of a Kosmic address, "altitude + perspective", introduced in chapter 1, returns here (ch. 1; ch. 2). The chapter ends with James Fowler's stages of faith, the line Wilber calls "spiritual intelligence" (ch. 2).

Chapter 3 turns to zone #1, states as experienced from inside. One experiences states, never structures (ch. 3). Vedanta's five natural states are waking, dreaming, deep sleep, witnessing and nondual, linked to gross, subtle and causal bodies ([The Psychic, Subtle, Causal, and Nondual](../concepts/psychic-subtle-causal-nondual.md); ch. 3). These states are available "to all humans at virtually any stage of growth" (ch. 3). Trained in meditation, they unfold as **state-stages** from gross to subtle to causal to nondual. Unlike **structure-stages**, they can be peak-experienced ahead of time (ch. 3). Daniel P. Brown's study of three Eastern traditions and John Chirban's of the Church Fathers both report that sequence (ch. 3).

### 4. States and Stages

This is the book's pivot. Spiral Dynamics, based on Clare [Graves](../people/graves.md)'s research, maps values from outside, so a student at one level can describe all eight but cannot live the higher ones (ch. 4). Zen's Ten Oxherding Pictures map state-stages from inside (ch. 4). Wilber says researchers long stacked meditative stages on top of the Western stages, and the problem "sat stalled for about two decades". It raised the question of how anyone 2,000 years ago could be enlightened (ch. 4). The answer was the **Wilber-Combs Lattice**, named for Wilber and Allan Combs, who reached a similar idea independently. It puts [Gebser](../people/gebser.md)'s structure-stages up one side and the states across the top (ch. 4). A person at magic, mythic, rational or pluralistic will interpret the same subtle-state experience of Christ magically, mythically, rationally or pluralistically (ch. 4). Interpretation is shaped by "the entire AQAL matrix", not just by stage (ch. 4).

The chapter then poses the "sliding scale" problem: if the world of form evolves, can anyone be fully enlightened? (ch. 4). The proposed answer is oneness with "**all states** and **all stages** that have evolved so far", argued fully in Appendix II (ch. 4). Evelyn Underhill's stages of the mystic path are state-stages and Fowler's are structure-stages, so they fit the lattice as its two axes (ch. 4). This explains, Wilber says, why advanced contemplatives can still hold conformist or ethnocentric views (ch. 4). He closes with four meanings of "spiritual": the highest levels of any line, a separate line, a peak or state experience, and an attitude ([Is Spirituality Developmental?](../concepts/five-definitions-of-spirituality.md); ch. 4). A long note sets out his disagreements with Spiral Dynamics (ch. 4, note 2).

### 5–6. Boomeritis Buddhism and the Shadow

Chapter 5 takes "boomeritis" as an example of pathology in a developmental line. Boomeritis is pluralistic "green" openness infected with egocentric "red" narcissism, first seen when pre- and postconventional protesters marched together in the 1960s (ch. 5). In American Buddhism, "the religion of no-ego" often became "the religion of express your ego" (ch. 5). Wilber argues that genuine meditative states got interpreted through a green stage and used to support it, and that "*none of this can be seen with the tools of Buddhism*" (ch. 5). Drawing on Traleg Kyabgon Rinpoche, he argues that meditation always needs a "View", a conceptual framework, so practitioners should make theirs integral (ch. 5). His advice: "NOTHING NEEDS TO BE CHANGED HERE", but supplement your path with stages, the eight zones and Integral Life Practice (ch. 5).

Chapter 6 adds the shadow, the disowned parts of the self. Anger I deny becomes a "you" I project, then an "it", a symptom such as depression (ch. 6). Wilber reads [Freud](../people/freud.md) as a phenomenologist: "Where it was, there I shall become" (ch. 6). Following Robert [Kegan](../people/kegan.md), healthy development makes "the subject of one stage" into "the object of the subject of the next" (ch. 6). Wilber's formula is that healthy development turns "I" into "me", and pathology turns "I" into "it" ([Repression](../concepts/repression.md); ch. 6). Meditation teaches letting go, but letting go of a feeling not yet owned deepens the split, so meditation alone does not cure the shadow (ch. 6). The cure is Integral Institute's 3-2-1 Process: turn the disowned "it" back into a "you" and then into "I", after which practices such as transmuting emotions work on authentic feelings (ch. 6). The chapter also introduces horizontal (states) and vertical (stages) enlightenment, and summarises the Upper Left as "**the 3 S's**": shadow, states and stages (ch. 6).

### 7–8. The We and the Right-Hand World

Chapter 7 asks how individuals relate to groups. Wilber argues that Web-of-Life lists, which stack families, nations and ecosystems on top of organisms, are "deeply, deeply, deeply confused" ([Systems Theory](../concepts/systems-theory.md); ch. 7). Drawing on [Whitehead](../people/whitehead.md) and Niklas Luhmann, he notes that an individual has a "dominant monad" but a group does not: "the we is not a super-I" (ch. 7). Groups have a "dominant mode of discourse", and unlike individuals they can skip stages (ch. 7). Zone #4 studies a "we" from outside (semiotics, Foucault's genealogy) and zone #3 from inside (hermeneutics) (ch. 7). Spirit also has three faces: 1st person (the great I), 2nd person (the great Thou) and 3rd person (the great It or Web of Life). Wilber argues that new-paradigm spirituality represses the second, the God to whom one surrenders (ch. 7).

Chapter 8 briefly covers the Right-Hand quadrants. Every state of consciousness has a brain-state correlate, but finding a meditation "G-spot" says nothing about whether what the meditator perceives is real (ch. 8). To test that, "*get into the stage or state from which the sentence is being written, and then look for yourself*" (ch. 8). Zone #5 is cognitive science and Maturana and Varela's autopoiesis, the organism seen from within (ch. 8). Zone #7 is Luhmann's view of society as communication, which leads to "Gaia exists, but as a club" (ch. 8). Zone #8 is classical systems theory, which, Wilber argues, becomes "subtle reductionism and monological imperialism" when it claims the whole picture (ch. 8). The chapter ends with the "myth of the given", the belief that reality is simply given to consciousness, which he says postmodernism refuted and most spiritual writing still assumes (ch. 8).

### 9. The Conveyor Belt

Wilber calls this "the single greatest problem facing the world in the interior quadrants" (ch. 9). On his estimate, 50–70% of the world's population is ethnocentric or lower, and the great religions own the beliefs of those stages (ch. 9). In research for a forthcoming book on terrorism, he says, the attackers all had "amber beliefs with red self": mythic beliefs and an egocentric center of gravity (ch. 9). Modern, rational culture puts an "orange pressure-cooker lid" on mythic religion. College students, for example, must either keep a mythic faith or give faith up (ch. 9). The root is the **Level/Line Fallacy**, "*the confusing of a level in a line with the line itself*" (ch. 9). In rejecting the mythic God, the Enlightenment threw out the whole spiritual line ([The Death of God](../concepts/death-of-god.md); ch. 9). This adds a new cause to the [dignity and disaster of modernity](../concepts/dignity-and-disaster-of-modernity.md). Only the "Big 3" of art, morals and science were differentiated, not a "Big 4" with spirituality, and science took over spirituality's ultimate concerns as scientism (ch. 9).

The cure is religion as a **conveyor belt**. Because religions hold the myths and the authority over them, only they can sanction rational and higher versions of their own message (ch. 9). Every stage is also a "station" in life that people have a right to stop at (ch. 9). Religions should also make contemplative states central, because, Wilber says, research shows that meditation speeds development through the stages (ch. 9). "*This jump from ethnocentric amber to worldcentric orange is the great leap that religions alone can help humanity make*" (ch. 9).

### 10. Integral Life Practice

Integral Life Practice (ILP) is the practical side of the theory: four core modules (an AQAL framework module, meditation, the 3-2-1 shadow process, and a "3-Body Workout"), plus auxiliary modules such as ethics, sex, work and relationships (ch. 10). The rule is to pick one practice from each module and do them together (ch. 10). Wilber claims ILP is the first practice based on all eight zones. By his count, [Aurobindo](../people/aurobindo.md)'s Integral Yoga recognised four methodologies and exercised three (ch. 10). The chapter ends lyrically, presenting AQAL as "a meta-theoria derived from a meta-praxis", a map to be dropped once Spirit is recognised, and religion as "institutionalized spirituality" (ch. 10).

### Appendixes

#### I. From the Great Chain of Being to Postmodernism in 3 Easy Steps

The traditional Great Chain runs matter, body, mind, soul, spirit. Its proponents, from [Plotinus](../people/plotinus.md) to Aurobindo, saw it as a nest in which each level envelops the lower ones (Appendix I). The traditions add [involution](../concepts/involution-and-evolution.md): Spirit first steps down into matter, then evolves back (Appendix I). Wilber keeps the experiences but questions the "*interpretations*" (Appendix I). Step one: matter is not the bottom rung but "the *exterior form* of *every* rung", so much that seemed "META-physical" is "INTRA-physical" ([Interiority](../concepts/interiority.md); Appendix I). Step two adds the cultural and social quadrants that postmodernity and systems theory study (Appendix I). Step three places subtle energies in the Upper-Right quadrant as hypotheses, with details left to Excerpt G (Appendix I).

#### II. Integral Post-Metaphysics

This is the book's central philosophical argument. Following Kant, Wilber defines metaphysics as thinking "that falls prey to the myth of the given" (Appendix II). The traditions' levels, he argues, "are not pre-existing structures lying around out there", like a building with ten floors (Appendix II). Any surviving version must meet modernity's demand "for objective evidence" and postmodernity's demand "for intersubjective grounding", and its levels must be "forms that have developed in time, evolution, and history" (Appendix II).

His positive account borrows Charles Peirce's idea of natural laws as habits. Levels are **Kosmic habits**: once enough people respond at a new level, the response settles into a structure every later human must develop through (Appendix II). The older the level, the deeper the habit is cut, like a canyon (Appendix II). This also answers the sliding-scale problem. Someone 6,000 years ago who had mastered all states and all the stages then in existence was fully enlightened, but the same attainment is not full enlightenment today (Appendix II). The definition is "**the realization of oneness with all the major states and major structures that are in existence at any given time in history**" (Appendix II).

Two samples of post-metaphysical thinking follow. First, every referent has a **Kosmic address**: an altitude and a perspective, for both perceiver and perceived (Appendix II). Ecosystems, for instance, are disclosed only from turquoise altitude, and Santa Claus exists in a magenta (magical) worldspace (Appendix II). A dog is a different object at each altitude: "*There simply is no such thing as “the dog”*" (Appendix II). Second, "the meaning of a statement is the injunction of its enactment": a claim about Spirit means something only if it specifies the practice that discloses it ([Paradigm as Injunction](../concepts/paradigm-as-injunction.md); Appendix II). On this view problems such as proving God's existence are not solved; they do not "arise in the first place". And there are levels of God, from magic to integral and beyond (Appendix II).

#### III. The Myth of the Given Lives On

Appendix III argues that spirituality was rejected by the humanities, not by science. In a "Great Left-Hand War", the Intersubjectivists (semiotics, structuralism, [Foucault](../people/foucault.md), [Derrida](../people/derrida.md)) defeated the Subjectivists (Husserl's phenomenology, existentialism), and dismissed introspection as caught in the myth of the given (Appendix III). Writers who try to ground mysticism in the new sciences "played right into the hands of the Intersubjectivists", since those sciences share the same flaw (Appendix III). Wilber then reviews more than a dozen books and a film, including works by Capra, Laszlo, Chopra, William James, Goleman, Varela and Thich Nhat Hanh. He credits each, then faults its failure to absorb the postmodern turn (Appendix III). The appendix ends by calling AQAL a "**psychoactive map**" that helps reverse the damage (Appendix III).

And you can study Spiral Dynamics till the cows come home, and you will NEVER have a *satori*. And the integral point is, if you don’t include both, you will likely never understand human beings or their relation to Reality, divine or otherwise.
>
> — ch. 1, “Integral Methodological Pluralism”

The "double death" of the contemplative traditions under modern and postmodern critique:

>
> — ch. 1, “Integral Methodological Pluralism”

The core of the Wilber-Combs Lattice: any state, interpreted from any stage:

> The point is that a person can have a profound peak, religious, spiritual, or meditative experience of, say, a subtle light or causal emptiness, but they will interpret that experience with the only equipment they have, namely, the tools of the stage of development they are at. A person at magic will interpret them magically, a person at mythic will interpret them mythically, a person at pluralistic will interpret them pluralistically, and so on.
>
> — ch. 4, “States and Stages”

>
> — ch. 6, “The Shadow and the Disowned Self”

The "startling new role" of the subtitle:

> All of which is good and beautiful. But precisely because of its ownership of the pre-rational heritage of humanity (and the pre-rational corpus of the great myths), religion alone can help its followers move from the pre-rational, mythic-membership, ethnocentric, absolutistic version of its message to the rational-perspectival, worldcentric, postconventional versions of its own message. *This jump from ethnocentric amber to worldcentric orange is the great leap that religions alone can help humanity make*.
>
> — ch. 9, “The Conveyor Belt”

The first step from metaphysics to post-metaphysics:

> Thus, what the premodern sages took to be META-physical realities are in many cases INTRA-physical realities: they are not above matter, nor beyond nature, nor meta-physical, nor super-natural: they are not above nature but within nature, not beyond matter but interior to it.
>
> — Appendix I, “From the Great Chain of Being to Postmodernism in 3 Easy Steps”

> A person’s realization today is not Freer than Buddha’s (Emptiness is Emptiness), but it is Fuller than Buddha’s (and will be even Fuller down the road)—and yet both the Buddhas of 2000 years ago and the Buddhas of today are *equally* enlightened by any *meaningful* definition of Enlightenment.

> The meaning of any assertion is therefore, among other things, the injunctions or means or exemplars for enacting the worldspace in which the referent exists or is said to exist (and where its existence can, in fact, be confirmed or refuted by a community of the adequate). Hence, in shorthand, **the meaning of a statement is the injunction of its enactment**. No injunction, no enactment, no meaning. That is, mere metaphysics.
>
> — Appendix II, “Integral Post-Metaphysics”

## What's contested

**What's strong.** The state/stage distinction gives a simple explanation of a familiar puzzle: why skilled meditators can hold conformist or ethnocentric views (ch. 4). The shadow chapter names a real gap between contemplative practice and psychotherapy (ch. 6).

**What Wilber himself concedes or revises.** He admits the earlier error of stacking states on top of stages, including in *Up from Eden* (ch. 4; Appendix II, note 4). He says *The Eye of Spirit* did not take the lattice into account (ch. 9, note 5). He calls his post-metaphysics "the most general of orientating generalizations" and "a set of possibilities", with "most of the detailed work" still to do (Appendix II). He also concedes that no philosophy can do entirely without *a priori* forms, only "the fewer, the better" (Appendix II). He keeps a "minimalist Eros" as an "involutionary given" (Appendix II, note 3), and of the Kosmic habits' "exact nature, we can debate that all day" (Appendix II). How far this escapes metaphysics is open to question.

**What remains contestable.** Several load-bearing numbers come with little sourcing. The population shares at each level are "a composite result of several sources" (Appendix II, note 6). The claim that meditation moves people about two stages in four years is "just a very general finding" (ch. 6). The terrorist profile comes from Wilber's own research for a forthcoming book (ch. 9). The tone is often sweeping. Putting it "in the bluntest terms possible", Wilber says around 70% of the world's population "is Nazis"; he explains that this is "simply an extreme way to state" that most people are at ethnocentric or lower levels and have not yet stably reached worldcentric ones (ch. 9). Appendix III judges more than a dozen books in a paragraph or so each. Wilber chose the light tone deliberately (Note to the Reader). Don Beck, co-developer of Spiral Dynamics, "strongly disagrees" with Wilber's view that it is not a complete model (ch. 4, note 2). On what is less new than it looks, see [From SES to Integral Spirituality](../concepts/from-ses-to-integral-spirituality.md).
