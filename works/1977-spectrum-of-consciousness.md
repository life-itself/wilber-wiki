# The Spectrum of Consciousness

**Year:** 1977 · **Format:** Book

## Description

Wilber's first book, written at 23. Maps Western psychology (Freud through humanistic
psychology) and Eastern contemplative traditions onto a single "spectrum" of identity
running from narrow ego to the whole cosmos. Established transpersonal psychology as a
field and made Wilber's name.

## In-Depth Overview

Written when Wilber was 23 and self-taught outside any doctoral program, *Spectrum*
is a mapping exercise: it lays out a "spectrum" of human identity running from the
narrowest possible sense of self (the persona, or masked ego, cut off from its own
shadow) up through the ego, the "biosocial bands" (the whole organism as conditioned
by society and symbol), the "existential level" (the organism as a whole, prior to any
social overlay), and finally the transpersonal bands culminating in identity with
Mind or Spirit itself — the whole Kosmos, no longer separate from anything. Wilber's
organizing move is to assign each major Western school of psychotherapy (psychoanalysis,
Jungian depth psychology, existential and humanistic/Gestalt therapy) and each major
Eastern contemplative tradition (Theravada Buddhism, Zen, Vedanta) to whichever band of
the spectrum it actually addresses, arguing that these schools aren't competing
theories of the *same* territory so much as accurate maps of *different* territories —
different, non-overlapping levels of felt identity.

That move made the book influential in establishing transpersonal psychology as a
credible field: it gave a structure within which contemplative claims and clinical
psychology could both be taken seriously without collapsing one into the other. It
also, in retrospect, contains the seed of the problem Wilber spent *The Atman Project*
correcting — because the spectrum is drawn as a single continuous line from ego to
Spirit, early *and* transcendent departures from ordinary ego-identity can look similar
on the map, which is exactly the conflation his later **pre/trans fallacy** argument
was built to prevent. Critics have also pointed to the book's heavy reliance on
secondary and popularizing sources over primary texts in the traditions it surveys —
an early instance of a critique (thin direct engagement with source material relative
to the scope of the synthesis) that follows Wilber's work throughout his career.
