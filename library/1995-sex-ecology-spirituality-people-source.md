# People index — who Wilber engages with, and how

Status: first pass, 7 of the book's ~25 significant recurring interlocutors
covered. Started 2026-08-22, per Rufus's steer that this is worth doing
now (not "someday" — see [docs/text-indexing.md](../../../docs/text-indexing.md)
for the broader indexing idea this is a concrete first instance of).

## What this is

Sex, Ecology, Spirituality is dense with named thinkers Wilber draws on,
argues with, or both. Chapter-level tags (as used in the Anki deck) don't
capture *who* recurs across chapters or *what Wilber actually says about
them* — this file does that, person by person, with real excerpted quotes
from the book (not paraphrase), so the material can seed things like the
[annotation system](../../../docs/annotation-system.md) later, or just be
read directly.

Format per entry: where they're discussed (by chapter, from a full-text
scan — main-body mention counts only, footnotes/references excluded),
Wilber's stance in one line, then verbatim excerpts with chapter +
approximate line number in
[`markdown/sex-ecology-spirituality-ken-wilber.md`](../markdown/sex-ecology-spirituality-ken-wilber.md)
as the locator (this book has no page markers in the converted text), then
a short synthesis of the argument.

## Jürgen Habermas

**Where:** ch. 4 (~1740–2050, introduced as primary source alongside
Wilber's own earlier work), ch. 5 (~2050–2530, evolutionary stages of
social integration), ch. 12 (~4820–5310, critique of instrumental reason,
alliance with Foucault).

**Stance:** Wilber's single most-cited living authority and closest
intellectual ally — "the world's foremost living philosopher and social
theorist" — used both to corroborate Wilber's own developmental scheme and
to supply the "three validity claims" that ground the whole book's
Big-Three/four-quadrant architecture.

> "The other is the work of Jürgen Habermas, whom many (myself included)
> consider the world's foremost living philosopher and social theorist.
> The conclusions of these two sources are in strong agreement, even
> though they were arrived at independently and from very different
> angles." — ch. 4, line 1741

> "Habermas's three validity claims, for truth (objects), truthfulness or
> sincerity (subjects), and rightness or justice (intersubjectivity),
> refer respectively to the Right half, the Upper Left, and the Lower
> Left... 'With any speech act,' he says, 'the speaker takes up a relation
> to something in the objective world [it], something in a common social
> world [we], and something in his own subjective world [I].'"
> — ch. 4, lines 1975–1981

> "Habermas begins with the observation that the *same structures of
> consciousness* (his phrase) can be found in the individual self (UL)
> and its cultural setting (LL)... an individual human being and its
> sociocultural environment evidence the *same* basic structures of
> consciousness (correlation of micro and macro)." — ch. 4, lines 2017–2025

> "Habermas, working from a different angle, arrives at the same
> conclusion, and he refers to it as the 'dialectic of progress'... of
> course there are new problems and new pathologies at each stage of
> development, but to take only the pathologies of the higher stage and
> compare them with only the achievements of the previous stage is
> perverse in the extreme." — ch. 5, lines 2471–2477

> "This is likewise what Habermas refers to as 'the totalitarian
> characteristics of an instrumental reason that *objectifies* everything
> around it, *itself included*.' In this respect, Habermas is in complete
> agreement with Foucault." — ch. 12, line 5169

**What Wilber does with him:** Habermas supplies two load-bearing pieces
of the book's architecture — the validity-claims triad (truth/rightness/
truthfulness → objective/intersubjective/subjective → the four quadrants),
and an independently-derived account of the stages of social/cultural
evolution (kinship → mythic empire → nation-state → planetary) that
Wilber treats as confirming his own earlier work in *Up from Eden*. In
ch. 12, Wilber also uses Habermas *against* the flatland reduction of
reason to mere instrumental/technical rationality — notably, showing
Habermas and Foucault, usually opposed, in agreement on that specific
critique.

## Joseph Campbell

**Where:** ch. 6 (~2707–2945, "Myth and Archetype" through the dedicated
"JOSEPH CAMPBELL" section), scattered elsewhere (ch. 5, ch. 7, ch. 11,
ch. 14).

**Stance:** Warm personal acknowledgment, sharp intellectual critique —
the clearest case in the book of Wilber's "pre/trans fallacy" diagnosis
applied to a specific, respected thinker.

> "There is no greater friend of mythology than Joseph Campbell, and I
> mean that in a good sense... Campbell has done more than any other
> person, with the possible exceptions of Mircea Eliade and Carl Jung, to
> champion the position that mythological thought is the primary carrier
> of spiritual and mystical awareness. I and countless researchers have
> drawn on his works time and again." — ch. 6, line 2887

> "And yet his position, I believe, is finally untenable, and can be
> demonstrated to be so using his own assumptions and his own
> conclusions. For his position is, in the last analysis, a form of
> elevationism." — ch. 6, line 2889

> "To begin with, Campbell openly accepts the essentials of the Piagetian
> system. That is, he accepts the fact that the basic motifs of
> mythological thought are produced by the infantile and childhood
> structures of preop and early conop, and he explicitly says so using
> Piagetian terms." — ch. 6, line 2891

> "And here Campbell commits the classic pre/trans fallacy. Since the
> prerational realms are definitely mythological, then Campbell wants to
> call the transrational realms 'mythological' as well, since they too
> are nonrational... So on the one side he lumps together *all*
> nonrational endeavors (from primitive mythology to highly developed
> contemplative encounters), and on the other side—the 'bad' side—he
> dumps poor reason." — ch. 6, line 2923

> "Campbell's dual definitions actually undo each other, and point
> instead to the inexorable conclusion: beyond mythology is reason, and
> beyond both is Spirit." — ch. 6, line 2943

**What Wilber does with him:** Campbell is the book's central worked
example of the pre/trans fallacy — conflating prerational (mythic,
childhood) structures with transrational (mystical, post-formal-operational)
ones because both are "nonrational." Wilber's method here is to use
Campbell's *own* premises (his explicit acceptance of Piaget's
developmental account of myth) against Campbell's own conclusion (that
myth, taken "as if," is itself a spiritual vehicle) — arguing Campbell's
argument actually proves the opposite: that "beyond mythology is reason,
and beyond both is Spirit," not that myth itself carries transpersonal
weight. Wilber is explicit that this doesn't erase myth's value — "the
real value of the Jungian/Campbell/mythopoetic approach" is honored as "a
rich source of one's own being and one's own roots" (ch. 6, line 3051) —
just not the elevated spiritual status Campbell claims for it.

## Jean Piaget

**Where:** ch. 6 (~2700–2870, developmental stages: magic → mythic →
concrete operational → formal operational), ch. 7, ch. 2, ch. 8.

**Stance:** The structural backbone — Piaget's stage sequence (and the
concept of egocentrism specifically) is the empirical spine Wilber builds
the whole book's developmental holarchy on, cited alongside Freud,
Habermas, and others rather than contested.

> "Piaget: 'The first stage is magical: we make the clouds move by
> walking... The second stage [magic-mythic] is both artificialist and
> animistic. Clouds move because God or [other] men make them move.'"
> — ch. 6, line 2703

> "In the broadest sense, *ego* means 'self' or 'subject,' and thus when
> Piaget speaks of the earliest stages being 'egocentric,' he does *not*
> mean that there is a clearly differentiated self or ego set apart from
> the world. *He means just the opposite*: the self is *not* differentiated
> from the world... Only with the emergence of a strong and differentiated
> ego... does egocentrism die down! The 'pre-egoic' stages are the most
> egocentric!" — ch. 6, line 2807

> "The *maximum* of egocentrism, as Piaget demonstrated, occurs in the
> primary or physical indissociation (the first fulcrum, where
> self-identity is physiocentric), because the entire material world is
> absorbed in the self-sense and cannot even be considered apart from the
> self-sense." — ch. 6, line 2821

> "Formal operational awareness transcends but includes concrete
> operational thought... Where concrete operational uses rules of thought
> to transcend and operate on the concrete world, formal operational uses
> a new interiority to transcend and operate on the rules of thought
> themselves." — ch. 6, line 2837

**What Wilber does with him:** Piaget's stage sequence (sensorimotor →
preoperational/magic → concrete operational/mythic → formal operational)
is used almost entirely non-polemically, as established developmental
fact, and is the single most-reused framework in the book — it's the
mechanism Wilber uses to explain *why* egocentrism actually decreases as
development proceeds (against the Romantic assumption that "primitive"
= "closer to unity"), and it's the shared premise both Wilber and
Campbell agree on before diverging over what myth's "as if" status means.

## Freud & Jung (their dispute over archetypes)

**Where:** ch. 6 (~2709–2721), a compact but pivotal disagreement Wilber
stages between them right before turning to Campbell.

**Stance:** Wilber treats their agreement (that archetypal/mythic motifs
are collectively inherited) as more interesting than their disagreement,
then sides with neither fully — closer to Freud/Piaget on function, but
explicitly not dismissing Jung's claim of spiritual content outright.

> "It is not often realized that Freud was in complete agreement with
> Jung about the existence of this archaic heritage. Freud was struck by
> the fact that individuals in therapy kept reproducing essentially
> similar 'phantasies'... 'I believe that these primal phantasies are a
> phylogenetic possession. In them the individual stretches out to the
> experiences of past ages.'" — ch. 6, line 2711

> "The question then centers—and here Freud and Jung bitterly parted
> ways—on the nature and function of these mythic motifs, these
> archetypes. Are they *merely* infantile and regressive (Freud), or do
> they also contain a rich source of spiritual wisdom (Jung)? Piaget,
> needless to say, sided with Freud on this particular issue. I have
> already suggested that I do not see these particular 'archetypes' as
> being quite the high source of transpersonal wisdom that Jung believed;
> but the situation is very subtle and complex." — ch. 6, line 2719

> "Freud's basic aim in therapy was therefore to reunite the I and the it
> and thus heal the split between them. His most famous statement of the
> goal of therapy—'Where id was, there ego shall be'—actually reads,
> 'Where it was, there I shall be.'" — ch. 6, line 2803

**What Wilber does with them:** Sets up a three-way split (Freud: myth is
merely infantile/regressive; Jung: myth carries real spiritual wisdom;
Piaget: sides with Freud functionally, but reframes the *mechanism* as
cognitive-structural rather than directly inherited) that frames the
entire Campbell discussion that follows — Wilber's own position lands
close to Freud/Piaget on the *developmental origin* of myth while
reserving Jung-style respect for myth's value once "divested of its
elevationism" (ch. 6, line 3051).

## Michel Foucault

**Where:** ch. 12 (~4959–5175, "the subject" as central problem of
modernity, alliance with Habermas on the objectifying "gaze"), ch. 13
(~5563–5617, biopower and the history of sexuality), ch. 4 (introduced as
one of four key theorists of culture/interiority).

**Stance:** A genuine ally on specific critiques (the objectifying gaze
of instrumental reason, the "sciences of man" as covert power), engaged
seriously rather than dismissed — closer treatment than Campbell's, more
skeptical than Habermas's.

> "Not only was it the central problem of the Enlightenment and the
> Romantic-Idealist rebellion, it has continued to be a central, often
> *the* central, topic in theorists from Habermas to Foucault to Taylor.
> (Foucault, for example, said, 'The goal of my work during the last
> twenty years... has not been to analyze the phenomena of power, nor to
> elaborate the foundations of such an analysis. It is not power, but the
> subject, which is the general theme of my research.')" — ch. 12, line 4959

> "These objectifying and systematic 'sciences of man' are in fact
> pseudo-sciences, according to both Foucault and Habermas, and
> pseudo-sciences driven by self-aggrandizing *power*." — ch. 12, line 5173

> "As Foucault ably demonstrates, the rise of modernity was marked, not
> so much by a repression of sexuality... as by an *obsession* with
> sexuality... 'Sex became the object of a major investment of
> signification, of power, and of knowledge.'" — ch. 13, line 5563

> "Foucault's work on 'biopower'—the modern rational Ego's obsession with
> sexual and bodily information as a way to control it—is merely one of
> numerous indicators [of Ego-rationalists building 'against the libido
> the bulwark of civilization']." — ch. 13, line 5617

**What Wilber does with him:** Foucault is used, alongside Habermas, as a
credible critic of Enlightenment "instrumental reason" and the
"dehumanizing humanism" of the human sciences — Wilber treats Foucault's
genealogy of the "sciences of man" and his account of biopower as
substantially correct diagnoses. The larger critique of Foucault
(postmodern antifoundationalism collapsing into "flatland," covered more
under Derrida/deconstruction in ch. 12–13) is present in the book but not
concentrated in a single passage the way the Campbell critique is — worth
a closer pass in a future update to this file.

## Plotinus

**Where:** ch. 9 (~3907–3970, the primary treatment — "The Great
Holarchy of Being"), ch. 10, ch. 12, ch. 13.

**Stance:** Unqualified hero — the historical figure Wilber treats as
having given the Great Chain/Holarchy of Being its first complete,
non-dual articulation, presented almost entirely through admiring
quotation from Plotinus and his interpreters rather than critique.

> "From Plato the torch of nonduality, the integrative vision, still
> intact, passed most notably to Plotinus (205–270 CE), who gave it one
> of the most complete, most compelling, most powerful statements to be
> found anywhere, at any time, in any form, ancient or modern, East or
> West." — ch. 9, line 3907

> "It is with Plotinus that the Great Holarchy of Being receives its
> first comprehensive presentation, although the notion itself, of
> course, goes back directly to Plato and Aristotle." — ch. 9, line 3925

> "'The One in Plotinus,' says Inge, 'is not unconscious, but
> superconscious; not infra-rational but supra-rational.'"
> — ch. 9, line 3951

> "For Plotinus (and Aurobindo), we find that *on the Path of Ascent*—or
> what Plotinus calls Reflux (return)—each successive level goes beyond
> and yet subsumes or 'envelops,' as Plotinus says, its predecessors—the
> familiar concept of development as successive holons... For Plotinus,
> all development is envelopment." — ch. 9, line 3969

**What Wilber does with him:** Plotinus is the historical anchor for the
book's central metaphysical claim — that reality is a holarchy running
from matter to body to mind to soul to Spirit, that "transcend and
include" governs its unfolding, and that this was arrived at
experientially (via contemplative practice) rather than speculatively.
Figure 9-1 in the book ("The Great Holarchy according to Plotinus and
Aurobindo") is explicitly built from Plotinus's own terms. Almost no
critique appears in this material — a useful contrast to how Wilber treats
Campbell or Hegel.

## Georg Hegel

**Where:** ch. 14 (~5873–6001, "The Unpacking of God" — Idealism's
achievement and collapse), ch. 13, ch. 8, ch. 12.

**Stance:** Deep respect for the insight, a clearly stated two-part
critique of the execution — structurally the same "acknowledge, then
diagnose the failure" move used on Campbell, aimed at a much larger
target (the entire Idealist movement).

> "'The Good,' says Hegel, 'the absolutely Good, is eternally
> accomplishing itself in the world; and the result is that it need not
> wait upon us, but is *already in full actuality accomplished*.'"
> — ch. 14, line 5961

> "And those were precisely the requirements met by Schelling and hammered
> out by Hegel. Nature and Mind are both taken up and integrated in
> Spirit, an integration that can occur precisely because the Spirit that
> is *awakened* in the *integration* is the *same* Spirit that was
> *present throughout* the entire process of unfolding and enfolding
> itself." — ch. 14, line 5949

> "The second failure: although profound intuitions and insights into the
> genuinely transpersonal domains were clearly some of the major, I would
> say *the* major, driving forces behind the Idealist movement, these
> intuitions and insights were expressed almost totally in and through
> *vision-logic*, and this burdened Reason with a task it could never
> carry... Particularly with Hegel, the transpersonal and transrational
> Spirit becomes wholly *identified* with vision-logic or mature Reason,
> which condemns Reason to collapsing under a weight it could never
> carry." — ch. 14, lines 5971

> "Would that Hegel had remained in poverty... But Hegel decided... that
> Reason could and should develop the tongues of angels. This would have
> been fine, *if* Hegel also had more dependable paradigms, more
> reproducible injunctions, for the developmental unfolding of the higher
> and transpersonal stages." — ch. 14, line 5975

> "This completely undercut any sort of idealist or spiritual view of
> evolution or manifestation in general. The first floor of the
> magnificent Idealist edifice crumbled, and the higher floors almost
> immediately followed suit (Idealism would survive in any viable form no
> more than a few decades after Hegel's death)." — ch. 14, line 5995

**What Wilber does with him:** Hegel gets credit for essentially
rediscovering the nondual insight independently — finite things as
inherently self-contradictory/unstable, driving Eros toward the infinite,
with the Absolute already fully present rather than a future arrival —
but is diagnosed with two failures: (1) mistaking vision-logic/mature
Reason itself for the transrational Spirit it was pointing at, rather
than treating Reason as a *pointer* requiring its own contemplative
"injunctions" (unlike, say, Zen's zazen) to verify; (2) historically,
Idealism's account of nature had no room for thermodynamics/entropy, so
when 19th-century physics undercut its "first floor," the whole edifice
collapsed within decades of Hegel's death — clearing the ground for
Feuerbach, Marx, and the "Dominance of the Descenders" that is ch. 13's
subject.
