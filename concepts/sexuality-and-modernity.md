# Sexuality and Modernity

## Wilber's View

Wilber's account of modernity's sexual obsession starts from a claim he borrows from
Foucault and then reinterprets: modernity wasn't marked chiefly by sexual *repression*
but by sexual *obsession* — an explosion of medical, psychiatric, literary, and
educational discourse about sex that began roughly a century before Freud. Wilber's own
addition is a diagnosis of *why*, and it follows directly from
[[flatland|the collapse of the Kosmos into flatland]]: once the [[ego-camp-and-eco-camp|
Ego and Eco camps]] both come to treat empirical nature as the only reality — the Ego
by reflecting on nature as the object of instrumental reason, the Eco by exalting
nature into Spirit itself — sexuality becomes the only available candidate for
"profundity" left standing, because it's the deepest layer either camp still has access
to. His verdict on Freud is characteristically double-edged: psychoanalysis calls
itself "depth psychology" but is, on this reading, actually the *shallowest* layer of
the self's "nine-layered onion" (matter, then libido, with seven transpersonal sheaths
above it) — it only looks deep by comparison with behaviorism, which didn't even get
that far.

The strongest part of the argument is the historical observation that "de-repression"
movements (Reich, "free love," Marcuse-inflected student radicalism, Norman O. Brown,
Bataille) kept promising a liberation that orgasm after orgasm never delivered — and
Wilber's explanation, that the *obsession itself* is a mark of modernity rather than an
escape from it, is a genuinely sharp diagnosis, not just a moralizing dismissal of
sexual liberation as such (he's explicit that he isn't denying real repression existed
or that some de-repression was warranted). What's more contestable is how much weight
the argument puts on a single master narrative — flatland collapse — to explain a
historically and culturally specific obsession that plausibly has other, more mundane
causes (contraception, urbanization, the medicalization of the body, the sheer novelty
of mass-printed discourse) that don't reduce cleanly to a story about the [[big-three]]
collapsing into one.

## In *Sex, Ecology, Spirituality*

> As Foucault ably demonstrates, the rise of modernity was marked, not so much by a
> repression of sexuality (although he does not deny that happened) as by an
> *obsession* with sexuality.
>
> — *Sex, Ecology, Spirituality*, ch. 13

> "Never, it seems, had so much attention been focused on every aspect of the body and
> every dimension of its sexuality. Sex became the object of a major investment of
> signification, of power, and of knowledge."
>
> — *Sex, Ecology, Spirituality*, ch. 13 (Wilber quoting Foucault)

> If nature is the fundamental and in many respects the final reality (according to the
> reflective Ego) and nature is the source of deep, ultimate, and often hidden truths
> (according to the Eco), then how better to unite them than in the belief that
> *sexuality holds the secret to human personality*?
>
> — *Sex, Ecology, Spirituality*, ch. 13

> The Tao reduced to the id: there is only the most notorious example of the collapse
> of the Kosmos into a flatland mononature, with sexuality the new and virtually sole
> (or certainly "most important") drive of the flatland world.
>
> — *Sex, Ecology, Spirituality*, ch. 13

> Approaches such as the Freudian were called "depth psychology" precisely as a
> *misnomer*: it was actually "shallow psychology."
>
> — *Sex, Ecology, Spirituality*, ch. 13

> ...the obsession with sexuality was not an escape from modernity but a mark of
> modernity.
>
> — *Sex, Ecology, Spirituality*, ch. 13

> In short, when monological thought became God, sex became God's obsession. And when
> monological thought likewise converted nature into God, sex became the key to human
> liberation and salvation.
>
> — *Sex, Ecology, Spirituality*, ch. 13
