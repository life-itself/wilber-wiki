# The Great Plenitude

## Wilber's View

Wilber leans hard on Arthur Lovejoy's *The Great Chain of Being* to give the
[[great-chain-of-being|Great Chain]]'s Descending half a name and a history: the
**principle of Plenitude**, summarized as "the greater the diversity, the greater the
Goodness." Where the Ascending path (see [[ascent-and-descent]]) sought the One by
withdrawing from the Many, the Descending or "Effluxing" path — Wilber traces it
through the *Timaeus*, Plotinus, and medieval nondual philosophers like Nicholas
Cusanus — found the One's Goodness *in* the Many's sheer variety: no possible kind of
being can be missing without diminishing Goodness itself, hence "there are no gaps in
nature." Wilber's most interesting move is historical rather than metaphysical: he
argues, following Lovejoy, that Plenitude wasn't just a background belief for
Renaissance and Enlightenment thinkers but an actual *research program* — an a priori
commitment (nature must be a graded, unbroken plenum) that scientists then went looking
for confirming evidence of, rather than a conclusion empirical discovery led them to.
The examples are genuinely striking: Bruno's plurality-of-worlds cosmology preceding
any evidence for it, Leibniz predicting microorganisms before Leeuwenhoek's microscope,
and the long, half-comic hunt for "missing links" between species that predates Darwin
by over a century.

Wilber uses this to make a bigger point later in the book: Plenitude was originally
only *half* the Plotinian equation (the Descending half, meant to be balanced against
Ascent), but once the Ascending God was rejected wholesale (see
[[death-of-god|The Death of God]]), Plenitude's "maximize diversity" imperative
became the *whole* picture and hardened into systems theory — see
[[systems-theory|Systems Theory and the Sciences of Complexity]]. The history-of-science
claim is worth some skepticism on its own terms: "a priori assumption preceded and
shaped the search" and "the assumption was the *sole* cause of the discovery" are
different, stronger claims, and Wilber mostly argues the former while sometimes writing
as though he's shown the latter — actual scientific practice (observation feeding back
into and revising the guiding picture) is messier than a clean research-program-first
narrative allows.

## In *Sex, Ecology, Spirituality*

> In order to understand the coming dominance of the Descenders, it is necessary to
> understand how the nondual system of Plato/Plotinus viewed the Descending Path of
> Efflux or Plenitude, which was only *half* of the equation, but which may be
> summarized in the modern phrase "Biodiversity is good."
>
> — *Sex, Ecology, Spirituality*, ch. 10

> Lovejoy demonstrates that wherever the Descending current was acknowledged—under the
> general name of the principle of Plenitude—it was always of the form: the Goodness of
> the One is reflected in the "variety of its parts"; the *greater the diversity*, *the
> greater the Goodness*. Maximization of diversity is, so to speak, what Plenitude
> seeks.
>
> — *Sex, Ecology, Spirituality*, ch. 10

> And the central point of this Great Holarchy or Great Chain of (Apparent) Being was
> summarized in the phrase: *there are no gaps in nature*.
>
> — *Sex, Ecology, Spirituality*, ch. 10

> Lovejoy demonstrates, quite convincingly I think, that many of the "scientific
> discoveries" from the Renaissance to the Enlightenment were not originally empirical
> and "surprising" discoveries, but were in fact *a priori* assumptions derived directly
> (and consciously) from the two main tenets of the Platonic/Plotinian doctrine of
> Plenitude: nature is graded in a holarchy of being, and there are no missing links
> *anywhere*.
>
> — *Sex, Ecology, Spirituality*, ch. 11

> "The change from a geocentric to a heliocentric system was far less momentous than
> the change from a heliocentric to an acentric one."
>
> — *Sex, Ecology, Spirituality*, ch. 11 (footnote)

> "Every discovery of a new form could be regarded, not as the disclosure of an
> additional unrelated fact in nature, but as a step toward the completion of a
> systematic structure of which the general plan was known in advance."
>
> — *Sex, Ecology, Spirituality*, ch. 11 (footnote)

> The doctrine of Plenitude as a research agenda: there was the Descending God of
> Infinite Efflux released from its confinement in the Middle Ages and set loose with
> all its delight in glorious diversity.
>
> — *Sex, Ecology, Spirituality*, ch. 11
