# Sex and Gender: Male and Female Advantage/Liberation

## Wilber's View

This section is the closest the book comes to explaining its own title, and Wilber
frames it as a truce between competing feminisms. Liberal feminism holds that men and
women are fundamentally equivalent in capacity; radical feminism holds that they
represent genuinely different value spheres — the male sphere agentic and
autonomy-oriented, the female sphere communal and nurturance-oriented. Wilber's move is
to relocate this disagreement onto his own architecture: what if radical feminists are
right about the *biospheric* component of sexual difference (physical strength and
mobility on one hand, gestation and lactation on the other — brute biological
"constants" he traces back to hominid hunting-band divisions of labor), while liberal
feminists are right about the *noospheric* component (once mind differentiates from
body, sex-linked roles are no longer biologically compelled, and equality before the
law becomes both possible and correct)? On his account these biological constants
started out as a fairly neutral **differentiation of function** — his worked example is
the shift from hoe-based horticulture (largely female labor) to plow-based agriculture
(which favored male strength and triggered a wholesale shift from Great-Mother to
Great-Father deity figures) — that historically hardened into **dissociation** and
status inequality, exactly the differentiation/dissociation pattern he applies
everywhere else in the book.

The genuinely careful part of this argument is the refusal to treat "biology" and
"oppression" as opposites: Wilber insists a difference of *function* only became a
difference of *status* through historical developments, not biological necessity, which
lets him affirm real average sex differences without licensing the conclusion that any
resulting inequality was natural or justified. What he explicitly defers, though, is
the actual reckoning: this chapter promises that a fuller review of the status of men
and women at each historical stage, and detailed engagement with liberal, radical, and
social feminist scholarship, will come in the announced but (at the time of this
book's writing) unpublished second volume of the series — so what's presented here is
the scaffolding for an argument about gender, not the argument's payoff.

## In *Sex, Ecology, Spirituality*

> At this early point, this differentiation or asymmetry does not appear to have been
> strongly evaluative—that is, one sphere was not particularly more important or more
> valued than the other. It appears to have been mostly a simple differentiation of
> *function*, not a massive differentiation of *status*.
>
> — *Sex, Ecology, Spirituality*, ch. 5, "The Emergence of Human Nature" (section "Male
> Advantage and Female Advantage")

> My claim is that the structures of human consciousness that I will be presenting in
> the rest of this volume are in fact gender neutral, that there is no fundamental
> gender bias in the deep structures themselves. *However*, in the course of historical
> development, these gender-neutral structures... became loaded with various factors...
> that biased some of these structures in an often specifically subjugating and
> certainly polarizing (or dissociating) fashion.
>
> — *Sex, Ecology, Spirituality*, ch. 5, "The Emergence of Human Nature" (section "Male
> Advantage and Female Advantage")

> ...one of the conclusions of volume 2 is that where women work the fields with a hoe,
> God is a Woman; where men work the fields with a plow, God is a Man.
>
> — *Sex, Ecology, Spirituality*, ch. 5, "The Emergence of Human Nature" (section "Male
> Advantage and Female Advantage")

> That initial differentiation of *function*, however, could be (and in many cases was)
> parlayed into a difference in *status*, with males dominating the public/productive
> sphere and women relegated to the private/reproductive sphere.
>
> — *Sex, Ecology, Spirituality*, ch. 5, "The Emergence of Human Nature" (section "Male
> Advantage and Female Advantage")

> But what if the radical feminists are right when it comes to the biospheric component
> of human beings, and the liberal feminists are right when it comes to the noospheric
> component? If this integration could be effected in a genuine fashion... we could
> simultaneously honor some of the important differences in sexual being—so emphasized
> by the radical feminists—and yet also insist on, and maintain, equality before the law.
>
> — *Sex, Ecology, Spirituality*, ch. 5, "The Emergence of Human Nature" (section "Male
> and Female Liberation")

> The point now is that, with the differentiation of the noosphere and the biosphere,
> the roles of men and women were no longer necessarily or automatically determined (or
> largely influenced) by biological factors (such as physical strength/mobility and
> birthing/nursing), factors that, one way or another, had largely *dominated* not only
> the relation of men to women but also of men to men, up to that point in history.
>
> — *Sex, Ecology, Spirituality*, ch. 5, "The Emergence of Human Nature" (section "Male
> and Female Liberation")
