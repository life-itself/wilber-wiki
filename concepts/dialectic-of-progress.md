# The Dialectic of Progress

## Wilber's View

The "dialectic of progress" is [[habermas|Habermas]]'s term, which Wilber adopts
because it independently confirms a claim central to his own developmental account:
every evolutionary advance solves the defining problems of the stage before it while
generating a *new* category of problem specific to itself — never in Wilber's reading a
frictionless net gain, but never a straightforward net loss either. He uses it as his
main weapon against what he elsewhere calls the retro-Romantic move: comparing only the
achievements of an earlier, "simpler" stage against only the pathologies of the current
one, a comparison he calls "perverse in the extreme" because it's methodologically
rigged from the start. Habermas's own worked example, which Wilber quotes at length, is
a phylogenetic needs-hierarchy running from the family/magic stage (the problem of
demarcating society from nature; power over nature as the new scarce resource) through
the mythic-imperial state (self-regulation of the social system; legal security as
scarce resource) to industrial capitalism (self-regulated exchange with external
nature; value as scarce resource) to a postmodern, vision-logic stage he speculates
about (self-regulated exchange with *internal* nature; motivation and meaning as scarce
resource). Wilber notes, and clearly finds significant, that this lines up closely with
Maslow's and Loevinger's independently-derived *individual* needs hierarchies — see
[[waves-and-streams]] for the general apparatus this is meant to confirm.

The argument does real work against naive progress-narratives and naive
decline-narratives alike, which is its strength: it structurally rules out both "things
just keep getting better" and "we've fallen from a golden age," since *every* stage
carries both a genuine achievement and a genuine new burden. What it doesn't obviously
license is the further, more contestable move Wilber makes elsewhere in the book — using
this pattern to rank stages as objectively higher or lower rather than merely
*different-with-different-tradeoffs*. A dialectic in which each stage both solves and
creates problems is compatible with reading history as net progress, net treadmill, or
genuinely incommensurable across epochs; Wilber consistently reads it as the first,
and the "dialectic of progress" framing itself doesn't settle which reading is correct
— that argument has to come from elsewhere in his developmental scheme.

## In *Sex, Ecology, Spirituality*

> Recall that one of the defining aspects of evolution is that it brings new and
> emergent possibilities and therefore new and potential pathologies. Habermas, working
> from a different angle, arrives at the same conclusion, and he refers to it as the
> "dialectic of progress".
>
> — *Sex, Ecology, Spirituality*, ch. 5

> Evolutionarily important innovations mean not only a new level of learning but a new
> problem situation as well, that is, a new category of burdens that accompany the new
> social formation. The dialectic of progress can be seen in the fact that with the
> acquisition of problem-solving abilities new problem situations come to
> consciousness.
>
> — *Sex, Ecology, Spirituality*, ch. 5 (Wilber quoting Habermas)

> Habermas's critique of Romantic regression is along the same lines as mine: of course
> there are new problems and new pathologies at each stage of development, but to take
> only the pathologies of the higher stage and compare them with only the achievements
> of the previous stage is perverse in the extreme.
>
> — *Sex, Ecology, Spirituality*, ch. 5

> With the transition to the sociocultural form of life... there arose *the problem of
> demarcating* [*beginning* to differentiate] *society from external nature*... Power
> over nature came into consciousness as a scarce resource...
>
> — *Sex, Ecology, Spirituality*, ch. 5 (Wilber quoting Habermas)

> The emergence of a new level solves or "defuses" some of the central problems and
> limitations of the previous stage (or else it wouldn't emerge), but it also introduces
> its own new problems and new *scarce resources*.
>
> — *Sex, Ecology, Spirituality*, ch. 5

> But any way we slice the evolutionary pie, there is where we stand today: on the verge
> of a planetary transformation, struggling to be secured by rationality and completed
> by vision-logic, and embedded in global-planetary social institutions.
>
> — *Sex, Ecology, Spirituality*, ch. 5
