# The Death of God

## Wilber's View

Wilber reads secularization not as reason simply outgrowing superstition but as the
final act of the [[ego-camp-and-eco-camp|Ego camp and Eco camp]]'s joint victory over
Ascent. His argument runs through Origen, the Church's greatest early theologian, who
solved the "problem of mythology" — a literal-minded scripture the young Church had
inherited and couldn't disown — with the allegorical method: any myth can be read as
literal, ethical, or allegorical, each level "higher" than the last, so that the myth
is simultaneously preserved (nothing is denied) and negated (nothing need be believed
literally). The Enlightenment philosophers inherited exactly this target: when they
tore down the *literal* mythic God — the anthropomorphic figure of tribal scripture —
they had good reason to (it deserved to go), and Nietzsche's "God is dead" became the
age's rallying cry. Wilber's real claim, and the one worth sitting with rather than
nodding past, is that the Enlightenment's target and Origen's/Plotinus's target were
never the same thing: the mythic God is prerational (a literalized, egocentric figure
that hasn't yet been through formal-rational critique), while the transrational
Godhead the contemplative sages pointed to had already, on Wilber's account, been
through and beyond rational critique. Because both were called "God" and both were
espoused (however differently) by mythically-situated cultures, the Enlightenment's
demolition swept away the transrational along with the prerational — this is the book's
recurring [[pre-trans-fallacy]] applied at civilizational scale. What was left standing,
once the Ascending God was gone, was only the Descended God of nature — see
[[great-plenitude|The Great Plenitude]] for the doctrine that then filled the
vacuum, and [[ascent-and-descent]] for the underlying polarity.

The weak point in this argument is one Wilber doesn't dwell on: it requires taking
Origen (and Plotinus, and Eckhart) as reliable representatives of what "mystical
religion" actually offers, rather than as an elite, philosophically sophisticated
minority tradition that was never what most premodern believers meant by God in the
first place. If mythic literalism *was* the operative content of religion for most
people across most of history — not merely religion's degraded, contingent expression —
then the Enlightenment's rejection of it looks less like collateral damage against an
innocent transrational bystander and more like an accurate diagnosis of what was
actually there to reject. Wilber's case depends on treating the contemplative core as
the *real* tradition and mythic literalism as an accretion around it; a critic could
just as easily run that ranking the other way.

## In *Sex, Ecology, Spirituality*

> "No more myths!" was the philosophical battle cry of an entire age, and it soon came
> to be summarized in Nietzsche's famous dictum, "God is dead."
>
> — *Sex, Ecology, Spirituality*, ch. 11

> And my conclusion will be that in throwing out a prerational, anthropomorphic, mythic
> God figure, the "modern West" also tossed out any transrational, nonanthropomorphic,
> superconscient Godhead. Gone was a mass of bathwater; gone too a precious baby.
>
> — *Sex, Ecology, Spirituality*, ch. 11

> The brilliance of this scheme is that it takes a prerational myth (literal) and
> reworks it at both a rational (ethical) and a transrational (mystical) level, so that
> "the myth" can be made to say whatever it is necessary to make it say, quite
> regardless of how its originators actually meant it... The myth is thoroughly
> preserved—and utterly negated.
>
> — *Sex, Ecology, Spirituality*, ch. 11

> Are we, quipped Thomas Paine, "to suppose that every world in the boundless creation
> had an Eve, an apple, a serpent, and a Redeemer?"
>
> — *Sex, Ecology, Spirituality*, ch. 11

> But with the rejection by modernity of the Ascendent God in virtually *any* form,
> there was only the Descended God left, the God of a marvelous and creative nature, a
> nature that was everywhere not to be denied, but a nature in which *alone* could
> salvation now be found. The doctrine of Plenitude and the Many completely eclipsed
> that of the Good and the One: God in any form was pronounced dead, and nature alone
> alive.
>
> — *Sex, Ecology, Spirituality*, ch. 11

> Reason, in reaction to myth, thus chose to look almost exclusively downward, and in
> that withering glance, the modern Western world was born.
>
> — *Sex, Ecology, Spirituality*, ch. 11
