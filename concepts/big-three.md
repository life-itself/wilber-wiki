# The Big Three

## Wilber's View

The **Big Three** is Wilber's shorthand collapse of the [[four-quadrants|four quadrants]]
into three domains: **I** (the Upper-Left, subjective interior — art, aesthetics,
self-expression), **We** (the Lower-Left, intersubjective — morals, culture, shared
meaning), and **It** (the Right-Hand quadrants together, since both are exteriors
describable in objective "it" language — science, empirical fact). He treats this as one
recurring pattern that shows up across an unusual range of traditions: Plato's the True,
the Good, and the Beautiful; Kant's three critiques (Pure Reason, Practical Reason,
Judgment); Habermas's three validity claims (truth, rightness, sincerity); even the
Three Jewels of Buddhism (Dharma as the ultimate It, Sangha as the ultimate We, Buddha
as the ultimate I).

His central historical claim is that modernity's real achievement was **differentiating**
the Big Three — freeing science from having to serve moral or religious ends, freeing
art from having to be doctrinally correct, and so on — which was a genuine advance over
premodern societies where the three were fused (church could dictate to state and to
science alike). The catastrophe was that differentiation curdled into **dissociation**:
instead of three legitimate, mutually respecting domains, the "It" domain (empirical
science) ran roughshod over the other two, a collapse Wilber calls **the Big Three
reduced to the Big One of it-language** — the engine of [[flatland]]. He identifies the
task of postmodernity, following Habermas and Weber, not as regressing to a
predifferentiated fusion (impossible and undesirable) but as *integrating* the
already-differentiated Big Three at a higher level — a task he assigns to
[[vision-logic]].

## In *Sex, Ecology, Spirituality*

> With reference to the four quadrants: because both of the Right-Hand quadrants are
> exteriors that can be described in it-language, I will sometimes count them as one
> major domain, the other two being the I-language of the Upper Left and the we-language
> of the Lower Left. And for simplicity's sake I will refer to these as the Big Three
> (I, we, it).
>
> — *Sex, Ecology, Spirituality*, ch. 4, "A View from Within"

> In the broadest sense this is Plato's the True (or propositional truth referring to an
> objective state of affairs, it), the Good (or cultural justice and appropriateness,
> we), and the Beautiful (or the individual-aesthetic dimension, I). The Big Three are
> likewise Kant's three critiques: the Critique of Pure Reason (theoretical it-reason),
> of Practical Reason or intersubjective morality (we), and of personal Aesthetic
> Judgment (I).
>
> — *Sex, Ecology, Spirituality*, ch. 4, "A View from Within"

> The differentiation of the Big Three thus degenerated, by the end of the eighteenth
> century, into the dissociation of the Big Three (a point made by Habermas)—which in
> turn allowed the Big Three to be rudely reduced to the Big One of it-language: the
> flatland, one-dimensional, monological systems theory of the representational and
> production paradigms, nightmares through and through, with all interior depth...
> reduced to being merely a strand in the endlessly flat and faded system.
>
> — *Sex, Ecology, Spirituality*, ch. 5, "The Emergence of Human Nature"

> What rationality had put asunder, vision-logic would unite. That, at any rate, is the
> potential and the promise and the struggle of postmodernity. What modernity
> differentiated, postmodernity must integrate. And if rationality did the
> differentiation, then postrationality must do the healing (and postrationality, I have
> been maintaining, is preeminently vision-logic).
>
> — *Sex, Ecology, Spirituality*, ch. 11, "Brave New World"
