# The Dignity and Disaster of Modernity

## Wilber's View

This is Wilber's recurring refrain for refusing both uncritical boosterism about
modernity and blanket Romantic condemnation of it. Modernity's **dignity**, following
Max Weber and Charles Taylor, was the genuine differentiation of the [[big-three|Big
Three]] — science (it), morals (we), and art (I) — into autonomous value spheres, each
finally free to pursue its own validity claims (truth, justice, taste) without being
subordinated to or policed by the others. That's real, hard-won progress on Wilber's
account, not an illusion to be debunked: it's the same move as "No more myths," the
liberation from mythic-imperialism and ethnocentric prejudice that comes with formal
operational, worldcentric cognition. Modernity's **disaster** is that differentiation
was never followed by integration: with no unifying center holding the Big Three
together, they didn't just separate, they *dissociated* — became "hyper-autonomous"
realms with nothing to say to each other, held apart by mutual disdain, exactly the
differentiation-going-too-far pattern Wilber applies at every scale in this book (see
[[repression]] for its individual-psyche version).

The elegance of this framing is that it lets Wilber hold two things at once that most
accounts of modernity treat as a package deal to accept or reject wholesale: he can
credit the Enlightenment's differentiation of value spheres as genuine emancipation
while still diagnosing exactly what went wrong, without needing either a triumphalist
or a declinist story. What's doing a lot of unstated work, though, is the assumption
that "differentiation followed by adequate integration" was ever really on offer as a
historical possibility rather than a retrospective ideal — Wilber's own account (see
[[ego-camp-and-eco-camp|The Ego Camp and the Eco Camp]]) suggests the flatland ontology
that caused the dissociation was baked in from very early in the Enlightenment's own
philosophical commitments, which raises the question of whether "dignity without
disaster" was ever actually achievable given the premises modernity started from.

## In *Sex, Ecology, Spirituality*

> We have seen that one of the great defining marks of modernity—what has been called
> its genuine *dignity*—was the clear differentiation of the Big Three, the
> differentiation of science (it), morals (we), and art (I), so that each could pursue
> its own course and establish its own truths without domination by the others.
>
> — *Sex, Ecology, Spirituality*, ch. 12, "The Collapse of the Kosmos"

> "Cultural modernity's specific dignity is constituted by what Max Weber called the
> differentiation of the value spheres in accord with their own logics."
>
> — *Sex, Ecology, Spirituality*, ch. 12, "The Collapse of the Kosmos"

> But if the *dignity* of modernity was the differentiation of the Big Three, the
> *disaster* of modernity would be that it had *not yet* found a way to *integrate*
> them. Indeed, with no unifying center, the Big Three had already begun to move into
> *dissociation*—to exist as radically separate, "hyper-autonomous" realms, with nothing
> to say to each other, bound only by a thoroughgoing and often mutual disdain.
>
> — *Sex, Ecology, Spirituality*, ch. 12, "The Collapse of the Kosmos"

> This was indeed part of the *dignity* of modernity, part of the liberating and
> enlightening move that strove, ideally, to free itself from ethnocentric prejudice and
> mythic imperialism, and the violence inherent in those shallower engagements. No more
> myths!
>
> — *Sex, Ecology, Spirituality*, ch. 12, "The Collapse of the Kosmos"

> These values—in particular, universal pluralism, altruism, and freedom—are what Taylor
> calls the three "hypergoods"... that no doubt constitute much of the true dignity of
> modernity. But no sooner had the rational Ego secured some notion of these hypergoods
> than it began not just to differentiate from other goods, but to alienate them,
> dissociate them, repress them, seal them off.
>
> — *Sex, Ecology, Spirituality*, ch. 13, "The Dominance of the Descenders"

> ...most of the aspects of modernity that many theorists have seen as positive and
> beneficial can in fact be resolved into variations on "No more myths"... and likewise,
> most of the aspects of modernity that have been harshly criticized by theorists can be
> resolved into variations on "No more Ascent!"
>
> — *Sex, Ecology, Spirituality*, ch. 11 (footnote)
