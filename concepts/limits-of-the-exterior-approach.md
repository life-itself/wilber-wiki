# The Limits of the Exterior Approach

## Wilber's View

This is Wilber's specific technical objection to systems theory, argued from *inside*
his own [[holons|twenty-tenets]] framework rather than against it. He grants that the
tenets are genuine "homolog laws" — the same dynamic patterns really do show up across
the physiosphere (A), biosphere (B), and noosphere (C). But he points out a structural
consequence that follows just from what it means for a law to hold across all three
domains: such a law can only be general enough to apply everywhere by describing what
A, B, and C have *in common* — which, since B and C both contain A as their base, means
the tenets are really laws of A (matter) that also happen to hold, in a thinned-out
way, for B and C. They can tell you evolution gets more complex; they can't tell you
why pride can be wounded, what honor means, or how to resolve an Oedipus complex — B
and C do further things (sexual reproduction, verbal communication) that aren't
derivable from the tenets at all. So a science built to cover *every* level
necessarily settles at the *least* interesting level — his memorable phrase is that
such a science "does not also cover the weakest noodle," meaning it can only assert
what's true even of the least conscious, least interior holons, and so tells us least
about the most conscious ones.

The sharpest part of the argument is his complaint that impressive systems-theory books
(he names Laszlo, and elsewhere Jantsch and Prigogine's popularizers) are "rich and
impressive" on the physiosphere, "somewhat" adequate on the biosphere, and go "pale and
anemic" the moment they reach culture and history — not because the authors are lazy,
but because that thinning-out is what the method itself guarantees. This is a fair and
specific critique of a real tendency in systems-theoretic and complexity-science
writing about mind and culture. It's less clear, though, that Wilber has shown
something *in principle* impossible for an exterior, empirical approach, as opposed to
something merely undelivered by the particular general-systems tenets he's built —
the chapter mostly asserts the limit via examples (poetry, Oedipus, honor) rather than
arguing why no future exterior science could in principle capture interiority; the
positive argument for that stronger claim is carried by [[interiority|the next
chapter's case for the within of holons]], not by this one.

## In *Sex, Ecology, Spirituality*

> In the previous chapter I said that holistic systems theories leave out the
> interiority of the holons they describe... they then subject these interiors to the
> same analysis as they apply to the surfaces, because they are trying to be empirical,
> and this produces some very unpleasant results.
>
> — *Sex, Ecology, Spirituality*, ch. 4, "A View from Within"

> But none of the twenty tenets—as crucially important as they are—describes
> specifically what is happening in B and C... The B and C qualities and functions and
> cognitions will follow the same tenets that also apply to A, but they will further
> follow other laws and patterns and actions not specifically derivable from the twenty
> tenets... There is nothing in the twenty tenets that will tell us how to resolve an
> Oedipus complex, or why pride can be wounded, or what honor means, or whether life is
> worth living.
>
> — *Sex, Ecology, Spirituality*, ch. 4, "A View from Within"

> ...the twenty tenets—by which I mean dynamic systems theory in general—are the *most
> fundamental* tenets of *all* of development, and therefore the *least* interesting,
> least significant, least telling tenets when it comes to B and especially C... nothing
> gets into systems theory that, to borrow a line from Swift, does not also cover the
> weakest noodle.
>
> — *Sex, Ecology, Spirituality*, ch. 4, "A View from Within"

> And the weakest noodles, the lowest holons, have the *least* depth, the *least*
> interiority, the *least* consciousness—so that a science of *that* is correspondingly
> a weakest noodle science. It is a science of surfaces.
>
> — *Sex, Ecology, Spirituality*, ch. 4, "A View from Within"

> ...one finds a very rich and impressive setting forth of something like the twenty
> tenets, and a rich application of them to the physiosphere and somewhat to the
> biosphere, but then the presentation, as it really explores the biosphere and then
> gets into the noosphere, becomes pale and anemic and thins out very quickly.
>
> — *Sex, Ecology, Spirituality*, ch. 4, "A View from Within"

> It takes more than imagination; it takes hallucination.
>
> — *Sex, Ecology, Spirituality*, ch. 4, "A View from Within"
