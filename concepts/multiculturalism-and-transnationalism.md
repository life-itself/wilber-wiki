# Multiculturalism and Transnationalism

## Wilber's View

Wilber reads both multiculturalism and the push toward transnational institutions as
symptoms of a developmental transition rather than as free-floating political programs
— a direct extension of the developmental-stages argument he builds with
[[habermas|Habermas]] in [[dialectic-of-progress|The Dialectic of Progress]]. On
transnationalism: he argues the global commons, world financial systems, and
international security have become structurally transnational crises that "no longer
respond to actions taken merely on the part of individual nations," but insists —
against purely technocratic readings of globalization — that no material-economic fix
can work without a "corresponding change in consciousness" among citizens willing to
surrender some national sovereignty, which he frames as requiring worldcentric
(egoic-rational, moving toward vision-logic) awareness rather than tribal or
mythic-imperial identity.

On multiculturalism his argument is more polemical, and it's the more interesting half
of the page. He credits the movement's actual tolerance and pluralism as a genuine
achievement, but argues it's self-contradictory on its own terms: multiculturalism
claims to reject "logocentric, rational-centric" dominance while its capacity to
tolerate other worldviews is itself rational through and through (only rational,
perspective-taking pluralism can hold space for views it disagrees with) — he calls
this a "misidentif[ication of] its own stance." His sharper charge is that
multiculturalism collapses "no perspective is final" into "all perspectives are
therefore equal," which is a non sequitur that erases the very judgment (rational
pluralism is *better* than ethnocentric intolerance) the movement needs to justify its
own tolerance in the first place. This is a real logical point worth taking seriously —
but it's also worth noting Wilber states the multiculturalist position in a fairly
uncharitable form to make the contradiction land; a more careful multiculturalism might
hold "no perspective is final in an absolute, view-from-nowhere sense" without thereby
denying that some perspectives are more adequate than others, which is closer to what
Wilber himself wants to say. The critique is sharpest against a strawman version of
relativism and less devastating against multiculturalism's more careful defenders.

## In *Sex, Ecology, Spirituality*

> The point is that all three of those concerns no longer respond to actions taken
> merely on the part of individual nations. Not one of those problems can be solved on
> a national level. They are literally transnational crises demanding transnational,
> worldcentric responses.
>
> — *Sex, Ecology, Spirituality*, ch. 5

> ...none of those physical and material and economic components *can be secured* in
> the long run without a *corresponding change in consciousness* among the citizens of
> the nations *surrendering* some of their sovereignty for the transnational good.
>
> — *Sex, Ecology, Spirituality*, ch. 5

> The "multicultural movement," which claims a universal tolerance of all cultures
> freed from the "logocentric, rational-centric, Eurocentric" dominance and hegemony, is
> a step in the right direction, with all good intentions, but ends up being
> self-contradictory and finally hypocritical.
>
> — *Sex, Ecology, Spirituality*, ch. 5

> In other words, multiculturalism is a noble, logocentric, and rational endeavor that
> simply *misidentifies its own stance* and claims to be not rational because some of
> the things *it tolerates* are not rational. But its own tolerance is rational through
> and through, and rightly so.
>
> — *Sex, Ecology, Spirituality*, ch. 5

> In other words, the "multicults" regress from "no stance is ultimate" to "every stance
> is equally acceptable," thus burying (and denying) their own otherwise accurate
> judgment that lesser stances and smaller perspectives are *unacceptable*.
>
> — *Sex, Ecology, Spirituality*, ch. 5

> Thus, that everything is relative does not mean nothing is better; it means some
> things are, indeed, relatively better than others, all the time.
>
> — *Sex, Ecology, Spirituality*, ch. 5
