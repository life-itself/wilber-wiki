# The Myth of Primal Ecological Wisdom

## Wilber's View

A recurring move in Eco-Romantic thought is to hold up tribal or foraging societies
as custodians of a lost "ecological wisdom" — a reverence for nature that modernity
severed and that we now need to recover. Wilber thinks this rests on a category error,
and it's the ecological instance of his more general [[pre-trans-fallacy]] (and of the
[[flatland|differentiation-vs-dissociation]] distinction that runs through the book).
Magic-era consciousness had not yet clearly *differentiated* the biosphere from the
noosphere — mind, body, and nature were run together in what cognitive anthropologists
call "indissociation" or "global syncretism." That undifferentiated condition can look,
from the outside, like a seamless integration of humanity and nature. But integration is
something you achieve *after* differentiation, by taking up and coordinating parts that
have first come apart. You cannot integrate what was never distinguished. So the
Romantic, Wilber argues, mistakes indissociation (a *pre*-differentiated state) for
integration (a *trans*-differentiated one), and mistakes a move backward for a move
forward.

The second half of the argument is empirical, and Wilber leans on writers who are
themselves sympathetic to tribal wisdom — Theodore Roszak, Riane Eisler, René Dubos,
Gerhard Lenski. Their own evidence, he says, shows that pre-modern peoples also
overgrazed, deforested, eroded, and hunted out their habitats; the damage was real but
local, limited by the scale of the means available, not by any special restraint. "Close
to nature" in the sense of *indissociated* does not translate into "ecologically
sound." The correct conclusion, on Wilber's reading, is that the difference between
tribal and industrial eco-devastation is a difference of power, not of wisdom: the *same*
ecological ignorance, now armed with far more dangerous means. His constructive point is
that the cure is therefore neither to strip away modern means nor to romanticize primal
ignorance, but to develop into a genuinely worldcentric, integrative mode of awareness
that can hold biosphere and noosphere together for the first time.

Two things are worth flagging as contestable. First, the "same ignorance, bigger means"
formula is asserted more than demonstrated — it does real work in the argument but rests
largely on a handful of secondary sources and a few striking examples (Mediterranean
erosion, tribes depleting a site and moving on) rather than a systematic survey.
Second, the argument is aimed squarely at a strong, sweeping claim ("tribal societies
*possessed* ecological wisdom as such"); it lands less cleanly against the weaker and
more common claim that particular practices, norms, or scales of living in particular
societies were in fact more ecologically sustainable, which Wilber grants in passing but
does not really engage.

## In *Sex, Ecology, Spirituality*

> It is that indissociation that is so often eulogized by Romantics, because, I believe,
> they **mistake indissociation for integration**. The magical-animistic structure, as
> lovely as it might appear to us jaded moderns, was not an integration of the biosphere
> and the noosphere, because these had not yet been differentiated in the first place.
>
> — *Sex, Ecology, Spirituality*, ch. 5, "The Emergence of Human Nature"

> *Lack of capacity* to devastate the environment on a large scale does not
> automatically mean *presence of wisdom*, let alone reverence for the environment. And,
> in fact, many tribes, as Lenski points out, simply remained at one location until they
> had ecologically depleted the area, and then were forced to move on.
>
> — *Sex, Ecology, Spirituality*, ch. 5, "The Emergence of Human Nature"

Wilber's key witnesses are writers otherwise committed to "primal/tribal wisdom." Roszak
concedes that

> tribal societies have abused and even ruined their habitat. In prehistoric times, the
> tribal and nomadic people of the Mediterranean basin overcut and overgrazed the land so
> severely that the scars of the resulting erosion can still be seen. Their sacramental
> sense of nature did not offset their ignorance of the long-range damage they were doing
> to their habitat.
>
> — Theodore Roszak, *The Voice of the Earth*, quoted in *Sex, Ecology, Spirituality*,
> ch. 5, "The Emergence of Human Nature"

> The correct conclusion, in other words, is that the primal/tribal structure *in itself*
> did *not* necessarily possess ecological wisdom, it simply *lacked the means* to inflict
> its ignorance on larger portions of the global commons.
>
> — *Sex, Ecology, Spirituality*, ch. 5, "The Emergence of Human Nature"

> The main difference between tribal and modern eco-devastation is not presence or lack of
> wisdom, but presence of more dangerous means, where the *same* ignorance can now be
> played out on a devastating scale.
>
> — *Sex, Ecology, Spirituality*, ch. 5, "The Emergence of Human Nature"

Returning to Roszak at length in Book Two, Wilber reads the whole Eco-Romantic project as
caught in a self-contradiction — it needs the primal state to be both parochial and
universally wise at once:

> ...he senses (and states) that the primal state was thoroughly parochial
> (egocentric/ethnocentric to the core), yet on the other hand it *must* be the repository
> of universal ecological wisdom, so he must figure out a way to eulogize the primal
> undifferentiated state and distance himself from it at the same time.
>
> — *Sex, Ecology, Spirituality*, ch. 13, "The Dominance of the Descenders"

> But since the primal structure (phylo and onto) contained neither *inherent* ecological
> wisdom (as he clearly demonstrates) *nor* a worldcentric/global orientation (as he also
> demonstrates), what exactly is it that we are supposed to recapture? The special form of
> primal ignorance?
>
> — *Sex, Ecology, Spirituality*, ch. 13, "The Dominance of the Descenders"
