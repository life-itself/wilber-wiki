# The Reconstruction of the Contemplative Path

## Wilber's View

Having argued (see [[validity-claims-of-mysticism|Validity Claims of Mysticism]]) that
contemplative practice can generate genuine knowledge via injunction, apprehension, and
communal confirmation, Wilber's next move is methodological: how do you turn a scatter
of culturally distinct contemplative traditions into a single reconstructive science,
the way linguistics reconstructs universal deep grammar from surface variation across
languages? His answer borrows directly from developmental structuralism (Piaget,
Kohlberg, Chomsky): every contemplative tradition has culture-bound **surface
structures** — its specific imagery, doctrine, and terminology — but Wilber's claim is
that beneath those, the traditions share **deep structures**, the actual invariant
*stages* of interior transformation the practices disclose, "just as the human mind
universally grows images and symbols and concepts (even though the actual contents of
those structures vary considerably)." He leans heavily on Brown and Engler's
comparative study of Tibetan Mahamudra, Theravadin Visuddhimagga, and Hindu Yoga Sutra
meditation manuals as the empirical case: independently written stage-maps that
converge, he argues, on the same underlying sequence — psychic, subtle, causal, and
nondual (see [[psychic-subtle-causal-nondual]] for the stages themselves).

This is a genuinely ambitious epistemological move — treating mysticism not as a body
of doctrine to interpret but as a developmental phenomenon to *reconstruct*, on the
model of a science rather than a hermeneutics. It gives Wilber a principled way to
respect real cultural difference (surface structures) without collapsing into either
relativism (every tradition equally arbitrary) or naive perennialism (every tradition
saying literally the same thing). The soft spot is how much is riding on "sufficiently
similar" doing real interpretive work: Brown and Engler's own conclusion is qualified
("the models are *sufficiently* similar to *suggest* an underlying common invariant
sequence"), and Wilber's confident four-stage summary compresses a great deal of
genuine disagreement among these traditions — about, for instance, whether the end
state is better described as absorption, cessation, or nondual recognition — into what
reads, in this chapter, as settled convergence rather than an ongoing and contested
research program.

## In *Sex, Ecology, Spirituality*

> ...to the extent that the contemplative endeavor discloses universal aspects of the
> Kosmos, then the deep structures of the contemplative traditions (but not their
> surface structures) would be expected to show cross-cultural similarities at the
> various levels of depth created/disclosed by the meditative injunctions and paradigms.
>
> — *Sex, Ecology, Spirituality*, ch. 7, "The Farther Reaches of Human Nature"

> Just as the human mind universally grows images and symbols and concepts (even
> though the actual contents of those structures vary considerably), so the human
> spirit universally grows intuitions of the Divine, and those *developmental
> signifieds* unfold in an evolutionary and reconstructible fashion, just like any
> other holon in the Kosmos.
>
> — *Sex, Ecology, Spirituality*, ch. 7, "The Farther Reaches of Human Nature"

> In the past few decades there has been a concerted effort on the part of many
> researchers... to rationally reconstruct the higher stages of transpersonal or
> contemplative development—stages that continue naturally or normally beyond the ego
> and centaur if arrest or fixation does not occur.
>
> — *Sex, Ecology, Spirituality*, ch. 7, "The Farther Reaches of Human Nature"

> The major [contemplative] traditions we have studied in their original languages
> present an unfolding of meditation experience in terms of a *stage model*: for
> example, the Mahamudra from the Tibetan Buddhist tradition; the Visuddhimagga from
> the Pali Theravada tradition; and the Yoga Sutras from the Sanskrit Hindu tradition.
> *The models are sufficiently similar to suggest an underlying common invariant
> sequence of stages*, despite vast cultural and linguistic differences as well as
> styles of practice.
>
> — Brown and Engler, quoted in *Sex, Ecology, Spirituality*, ch. 7, "The Farther
> Reaches of Human Nature"

> ...each possesses a deep structure (basic defining pattern) that is culturally
> invariant but with surface structures (manifestations) that are culturally
> conditioned and molded; and each has a new and higher form of possible pathology.
>
> — *Sex, Ecology, Spirituality*, ch. 7, "The Farther Reaches of Human Nature"
