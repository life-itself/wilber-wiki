# Erik Erikson

## Wilber's View

Erikson gets credit for a genuine disciplinary shift — psychoanalysis, in Wilber's
account, was reductionistic in the specific sense of treating adult problems as always
traceable back to early-childhood fixation, and Erikson's "psychosocial stages"
undermined that by insisting later life stages (adolescence, adulthood, old age) bring
their own genuinely new tasks and crises, not just replays of infantile conflict.

A detail worth knowing if you've read Wilber elsewhere: his own term **centaur** —
which shows up throughout his work for the mature, integrated mind-body self at the
top of the personal (pre-transpersonal) range — is borrowed directly from Erikson, who
used it for the same mind-body integration. It's a small provenance fact easy to miss
since Wilber uses "centaur" as though it were his own coinage in most other contexts.

Wilber's one reservation: Erikson's later stages don't cleanly separate vertical
development (genuinely higher structural stages) from horizontal "seasons of life"
(age-appropriate concerns that don't represent a higher level of consciousness) — a
conflation Wilber says he's simply accepted and placed on the charts at their
approximate position, rather than resolved.

## In *Integral Psychology*

> Erikson, coming from within the psychoanalytic tradition, posed such a profoundly
> far-reaching extension of its concepts that it actually helped to undermine
> psychoanalytic reductionism.
— *Integral Psychology*, ch. 4

> His "psychosocial stages," ranging from birth through adolescence to old age,
> struck an immediately sympathetic chord not only with the public but with many
> other researchers—he was clearly on to something of importance.
— *Integral Psychology*, ch. 4

> In Erikson's scheme, quite reminiscent of the "seven ages of a person," there are
> seven or eight major ages (or stages) of a person's life...Echoing a truth that was
> already beginning to surface from Baldwin's and Piaget's studies (and which was
> explicit in the German Idealists' vision, which greatly influenced both Baldwin and
> Piaget), each stage of development sees a different world—with different needs,
> different tasks, different dilemmas, different problems and pathologies.
— *Integral Psychology*, ch. 4

> Erikson's highest stages were not quite transpersonal (they were often horizontal
> unfoldings of a personal sort).
— *Integral Psychology*, ch. 4

> ..."Centaur" is a term used by Erikson to denote a mature mind-and-body integration,
> where "human mind" and "animal body" are harmoniously one.
— *Integral Psychology*, ch. 4
