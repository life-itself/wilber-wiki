# James Mark Baldwin

## Wilber's View

Baldwin (1861-1934) gets the single strongest individual endorsement of any researcher
in *Integral Psychology* — Wilber calls him "the most pivotal" of the modern
integral pioneers and floats that history might rank him "America's greatest
psychologist." The claim being made is specific: not just that Baldwin was early, but
that he was more genuinely integral than the researchers who built on him and became
better known. Piaget and Kohlberg both inherited core concepts directly from Baldwin
(egocentrism, developmental stage itself, the moral-stage sequence) without fully
inheriting his refusal to collapse everything into cognitive development — Wilber's
gloss is that Baldwin traced development across aesthetic, moral, and scientific
domains simultaneously (his own version of the [[big-three|Big Three]]) precisely
because he treated them as irreducible to one another, where his successors mostly
specialized in one.

Baldwin also gets used as Wilber's explanation for a historical puzzle: why does a
figure this foundational have to be reintroduced at all, rather than being a household
name? The answer given is basically a flatland story — positivism and behaviorism
hardened into dogma shortly after Baldwin's death and pushed integral approaches out of
the field, taking his reputation with them. That's a claim worth treating as Wilber's
own historical narrative rather than settled fact: it fits his broader argument
(flatland as the dominant 20th-century intellectual pathology) a little too neatly to
be purely incidental, though the underlying claim about Piaget's and Kohlberg's actual
intellectual debts to Baldwin is separately corroborated here by quoting Kohlberg
directly, not just asserted.

## In *Integral Psychology*

> Of the four, James Mark Baldwin (1861-1934) is the most pivotal, and history might
> well find him to be America's greatest psychologist. A contemporary of James and
> Peirce, Baldwin forged an integral psychology and philosophy that is only now being
> recognized for its scope and profundity.
— *Integral Psychology*, ch. 7

> He was the first great developmental psychologist in modern history; he was the
> first to clearly define a stage of development; he sought to integrate introspective
> phenomenology with scientific evolutionary epistemology; he believed that the three
> great modes of experience were aesthetic, moral, and scientific (the Big Three!), and
> he proposed detailed developmental stages in each of those domains (in other words,
> he was one of the first to trace development in all quadrants); he was also one of
> the first to outline stages of religious development.
— *Integral Psychology*, ch. 7

> The only reason his name is a not a household word is that, shortly after his death,
> the positivist and behaviorist schools would raise flatland to a dogmatic belief, and
> integral studies of any sort were scrubbed from the curriculum.
— *Integral Psychology*, ch. 7

> Baldwin came to see consciousness as developing through a half-dozen qualitatively
> distinct stages or levels of consciousness...the prelogical (similar to
> sensorimotor), the quasilogical (preop and early conop), the logical (formop), the
> extralogical (vision-logic), and finally, the hyperlogical, which we might call
> supralogical or translogical, for it represents a satori-like nondual awareness that
> transcends the subject and object dualism.
— *Integral Psychology*, ch. 7

> But unlike Piaget, Baldwin's genius was his integral vision: he refused to reduce all
> development to cognitive development, which is why, as an overall system, Baldwin's
> is much more credible and enduring, as John Broughton and others have pointed out.
— *Integral Psychology*, ch. 7

> As I read more deeply into Baldwin, I realized that Piaget had derived all the basic
> ideas with which he started in the twenties from Baldwin: assimilation,
> accommodation, schema, and adualism, 'egocentricity,' or undifferentiated character
> of the child's mind.
— Kohlberg, quoted in *Integral Psychology*, ch. 7
