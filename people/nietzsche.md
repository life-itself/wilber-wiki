# Friedrich Nietzsche

## Wilber's View

Nietzsche appears in *SES* less as an interlocutor in his own right than as the
headwaters of two currents Wilber treats with real ambivalence. First, genealogically:
Wilber repeatedly names Nietzsche as the starting point of the "postmodern
poststructuralist" lineage — from Nietzsche and Bataille through Foucault, Derrida, and
Lyotard — that supplies both his most useful ally (the "genealogy" method, borrowed via
Charles Taylor, for exposing hidden pragmatic contradictions in universalist moral
theories) and his sharpest target (deconstructive [[postmodernism]]'s slide into
nihilism). Second, Nietzsche's most famous line — "God is dead" — gets used by Wilber
as the crisp summary of the Enlightenment's whole "No more myths!" posture toward the
Age of Mythology, the philosophical mood that made room for reason's "dignity" but also
set up its later "disaster." And in one of his more sympathetic asides, Wilber places
Nietzsche among the philosophers whose intense, even reckless exploration of "limit
experiences" and depths — despite themselves being creatures of rationality's own
overreach — is what actually kept the door open to mysticism and interiority once
positivism had tried to close it. He also traces a separate line of influence, via
Schelling and Schopenhauer, running Nietzsche onward to Bergson, Heidegger, and Sartre —
placing him at the head of a whole "will" tradition modernity's other critics grew out
of.

## In *Sex, Ecology, Spirituality*

> "No more myths!" was the philosophical battle cry of an entire age, and it soon came
> to be summarized in Nietzsche's famous dictum, "God is dead."
>
> — *Sex, Ecology, Spirituality*, ch. 11, "Brave New World"

> ...the more one examines the motives—what Nietzsche would call the 'genealogy'—of these
> theories, the stranger they appear. It seems that they are motivated by the strongest
> moral ideals, such as freedom, altruism, and universalism... And yet what these ideals
> drive the theorists toward is a denial of all such goods.
>
> — Charles Taylor, quoted in *Sex, Ecology, Spirituality*, ch. 1, "The Web of Life"

> From Nietzsche to Bataille to Foucault, from Heidegger to Derrida to Lyotard, the
> critics have continued their assault, with the postmodern poststructuralists being
> merely the most recent, although possibly the loudest, of the long line of
> antimodernists.
>
> — *Sex, Ecology, Spirituality*, ch. 11, "Brave New World"

> In his idea of Eros as unlimited activity, he set off a whole line of "will"
> theorists, from Schopenhauer to Bergson to Nietzsche. Paul Tillich traces this line of
> influence: "The most important pupil of Schopenhauer was Nietzsche. The line then runs
> from Nietzsche to Bergson, the French voluntarist, Heidegger and Sartre, and to
> Whitehead, the great metaphysician of our century—all of this came from Schelling."
>
> — *Sex, Ecology, Spirituality*, ch. 13, "The Dominance of the Descenders" (on Schelling, quoting Paul Tillich)
