# Michel Foucault

## Wilber's View

Foucault gets a genuinely serious hearing in *Sex, Ecology, Spirituality* — closer,
critical engagement than the near-hagiography given to [[plotinus|Plotinus]], but far
warmer than the pre/trans-fallacy treatment of [[campbell|Campbell]]. Wilber's Foucault
is centrally the theorist of "the subject" — Foucault's own gloss (quoted directly) is
that his life's work was never really about power as such but about the subject, "the
general theme of my research." Wilber uses two of Foucault's specific arguments almost
without qualification: the diagnosis of the "sciences of man" as pseudo-sciences that
reduce human beings to objects of information rather than subjects in communication
(what Foucault called the birth of "the Age of Man"), and the account of **biopower** —
modernity's obsessive turn toward sexuality and the body as objects of knowledge and
control, developed at length in ch. 13 in connection with Wilber's own account of the
Ego's repression of the biosphere.

The most striking move Wilber makes with Foucault is recruiting him as an *ally of*
[[habermas|Habermas]] — two thinkers usually read as opposed (Foucault the
poststructuralist skeptic of grand narratives, Habermas the defender of a critical
theory of communicative reason) turn out, on Wilber's reading, to agree completely that
instrumental reason's "objectifying gaze" is the central pathology of modernity. Wilber
is also careful to track Foucault's own self-correction: the early "archaeological"
Foucault, who bracketed both truth and meaning to describe discourse as pure systematic
behavior, later recanted that approach as "arrogant" and folded meaning back in. The
larger critique of Foucault that Wilber develops elsewhere — postmodern
antifoundationalism collapsing, if pushed too far, into its own kind of "flatland" — is
present in the book but less concentrated than the sympathetic material excerpted here.

## In *Sex, Ecology, Spirituality*

> Not only was it the central problem of the Enlightenment and the Romantic-Idealist
> rebellion, it has continued to be a central, often *the* central, topic in theorists
> from Habermas to Foucault to Taylor. (Foucault, for example, said, "The goal of my
> work during the last twenty years... has not been to analyze the phenomena of power,
> nor to elaborate the foundations of such an analysis. It is not power, but the
> subject, which is the general theme of my research.")
>
> — *Sex, Ecology, Spirituality*, ch. 12

> These objectifying and systematic "sciences of man" are in fact pseudo-sciences,
> according to both Foucault and Habermas, and pseudo-sciences driven by
> self-aggrandizing *power*.
>
> — *Sex, Ecology, Spirituality*, ch. 12

> And thus was born what Foucault called "the Age of Man"—the merely "objective" study
> of humans. And in a beautiful phrase that would summarize the net effect of the
> entire Enlightenment paradigm... Foucault points out that with the "Age of Man,"
> humans became "the object of information, never the subject in communication."
>
> — *Sex, Ecology, Spirituality*, ch. 12

> This is likewise what Habermas refers to as "the totalitarian characteristics of an
> instrumental reason that *objectifies* everything around it, *itself included*." In
> this respect, Habermas is in complete agreement with Foucault.
>
> — *Sex, Ecology, Spirituality*, ch. 12

> As Foucault ably demonstrates, the rise of modernity was marked, not so much by a
> repression of sexuality... as by an *obsession* with sexuality... Never, says
> Foucault, had an epoch's discourse been so dominated by sexuality in so many domains.
> "Never, it seems, had so much attention been focused on every aspect of the body and
> every dimension of its sexuality. Sex became the object of a major investment of
> signification, of power, and of knowledge."
>
> — *Sex, Ecology, Spirituality*, ch. 13

> And let us immediately note that Foucault eventually abandoned archaeology as an
> exclusive methodology precisely because archaeology was itself merely a *part* of the
> larger holon of social practices: not wholes, but whole/parts.
>
> — *Sex, Ecology, Spirituality*, ch. 4
