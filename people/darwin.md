# Charles Darwin

## Wilber's View

Wilber's treatment of Darwin is a deliberate demotion, and he spells out exactly what
he thinks Darwin did and didn't accomplish. What Darwin did *not* do, in Wilber's
account, is discover evolution — the idea that reality unfolds developmentally through
time was already "in the air" for a century before him, out of Leibniz, Schelling, and
the whole "developmental philosophy" tradition that treated evolution as Spirit's own
self-unfolding (Eros). What Darwin did do was supply meticulous empirical evidence,
backed by the mechanism of natural selection (co-discovered with Wallace), for the
continuity of species — a genuinely novel and important scientific contribution, but a
much narrower one than "evolution" itself. Wilber's real charge against the Darwinian
legacy is what it did to that older tradition: by explaining speciation without any
appeal to a directional, self-transcending drive, Darwinism let science quietly drop
Eros from the picture altogether, and let a generation of scientists believe they'd
explained away any need for it. That, for Wilber, is Darwin's lasting effect — not a
discovery but an *obscuring*: "a massive obscurantism" that took over a century to see
through. He's careful to note the irony that Darwin himself "notoriously wavered" on
the point, and that Wallace, his co-discoverer, explicitly did *not* think natural
selection could replace a directional, spiritual reading of evolution.

## In *Sex, Ecology, Spirituality*

> The idea of the continuity of life—the web of life, the tree of life, the "no gaps in
> nature" view—was at least as old as Plato and Aristotle, and... there was precisely
> nothing new or unusual in Darwin's presentation of the continuous tree of life.
>
> — *Sex, Ecology, Spirituality*, ch. 1, "The Web of Life"

> The lasting contribution of Darwin's theory, then, was not that it discovered a
> mechanism for macroevolution, for it did not; rather, it obscured for over a century
> the fact that a genuine theory of evolution demands something resembling Eros. Darwin's
> *lasting* contribution was primarily a massive obscuratanism. Scientists all cheery and
> self-content began to scrub the universe clean of anything resembling love and its
> all-encompassing embrace, and all congratulated themselves on yet another victory for
> truth (Wallace, as is well known, did *not* think natural selection could replace Eros;
> evolution was itself, he thought, "the mode and manner of Spirit's creation," and
> Darwin himself notoriously wavered).
>
> — *Sex, Ecology, Spirituality*, ch. 13, "The Dominance of the Descenders"

> But it wasn't the Darwinian "revolution" that would play most decisively into the
> hands of the Descenders. After all, the Darwinists could always be seen... as simply
> supplying empirical evidence for a scheme already known and accepted, namely, evolution
> as God-in-the-making... Darwin was *never* the problem. It was Carnot, Clausius, and
> Kelvin.
>
> — *Sex, Ecology, Spirituality*, ch. 14, "The Unpacking of God"

> When Darwin came along and dutifully supplied some of the empirical evidence for
> biological evolution, it shocked nobody except the remnants of the mythic believers in
> the literal Genesis myth; but they were already shocked by what Schelling and other
> developmentalists were doing anyway.
>
> — *Sex, Ecology, Spirituality*, ch. 13, "The Dominance of the Descenders"
