# Carl Jung

## Wilber's View

Jung is the other pole of the ch. 6 dispute Wilber stages with [[freud|Freud]] over the
mythic archetypes — and Wilber gives the disagreement a name that becomes a load-bearing
concept for the whole book, the [[pre-trans-fallacy|pre/trans fallacy]]: **Freud was a
reductionist, Jung an elevationist, the two sides of the fallacy.** Both agreed the archetypal, mythic motifs that recur
across cultures are collectively inherited (a point of real agreement usually missed);
where they split was over what that inheritance *means*. Freud read it as merely
infantile and regressive. Jung read it as a genuine repository of spiritual and
transpersonal wisdom — and it's that second move Wilber contests, on the grounds that
Jung, like [[campbell|Campbell]] after him, collapses the *prerational* (childhood,
archaic, "next to the instincts") into the *transrational* (mystical, post-rational)
because both merely look "nonrational" from the standpoint of ordinary waking reason.
Wilber is careful about the shape of his own disagreement: he doesn't deny that
archetypal material deserves serious engagement — "it is altogether necessary to
contact and befriend them" — only that engaging with them is a *different* undertaking
from mystical realization, and that Jung's own account of the archetypes as lying "next
to the instincts" actually supports treating them as *deep* rather than *high*: proceed
in the direction the archetypes point, Wilber argues, and eventually you reach not
Spirit but atoms.

Wilber's verdict lands, structurally, in the same place as his verdict on Campbell (who
he treats as extending Jung's move) — real respect for what Jung recovered (a rich,
collectively-inherited symbolic inheritance worth "befriending"), paired with a sharp
denial that this inheritance carries the elevated transpersonal status Jung claimed for
it.

## In *Sex, Ecology, Spirituality*

> Freud was a reductionist, Jung an elevationist—the two sides of the pre/trans
> fallacy. And the point is that they are *both* half right and half wrong. A good
> deal of neurosis is indeed a fixation/regression to prerational states, states that
> are not to be glorified. On the other hand, mystical states do indeed exist, beyond
> (not beneath) rationality, and those states are not to be reduced.
>
> — *Sex, Ecology, Spirituality*, ch. 5

> Jung and his followers, of course, often take this route, and are forced to read a
> deeply transpersonal and spiritual status into states that are merely indissociated
> and undifferentiated and actually lacking any sort of integration at all.
>
> — *Sex, Ecology, Spirituality*, ch. 5

> This directly brings us... to the work of Carl Jung and his conclusion that the
> essential forms and motifs of the world's great mythologies—the "archaic forms" or
> "archetypes"—are collectively inherited in the individual psyche of each of us. It is
> not often realized that Freud was in complete agreement with Jung about the existence
> of this archaic heritage.
>
> — *Sex, Ecology, Spirituality*, ch. 6

> But as for the images and symbols and early concepts themselves, as for these
> "archetypes," they lie in the direction of downward, not upward (shallower, not
> deeper). This is why, I believe, however Jung variously defined the archetypes, he
> always maintained they were "next to the instincts," or the "instincts' image of
> themselves." ... And proceeding in the *direction* of the archetypes we therefore
> eventually run into, not Spirit, but atoms.
>
> — *Sex, Ecology, Spirituality*, ch. 6

> Jung: "The deeper layers of the psyche lose their individual uniqueness as they
> retreat farther and farther into darkness. 'Lower down' they become increasingly
> extinguished in the body's materiality, i.e., in chemical substances."
>
> — Jung, quoted in *Sex, Ecology, Spirituality*, ch. 6

> And this, I believe, is behind Jung's dual stance toward the archetypes: it is
> altogether necessary to contact and befriend them, but it is finally necessary to
> *differentiate* and *individuate* from them, break them of their power over us. In
> other words, befriend the images, rob them of their worldview.
>
> — *Sex, Ecology, Spirituality*, ch. 6
