# Baruch Spinoza

## Wilber's View

Spinoza does double duty in *SES*. In the book's early chapters he's cited, alongside
[[whitehead|Whitehead]], Leibniz, and Aurobindo, among the philosophers who recognized
that every holon has an *interior* — that "consciousness" (Spinoza's own word is
"cognition," paired with "extension" for the exterior view of the same event) isn't
something added to matter from outside but present, in some degree, all the way down.
That's Spinoza the epistemologist of the [[holons|within of things]].

The larger and more structurally important role comes in the book's history-of-Western-
philosophy chapters (13–14), where Spinoza becomes the paradigm case of the pure
**Eco** or **Descended** path — the philosophical pole opposite Fichte's "infinite
Subject." Where Fichte's Ego chases pure Ascent, transcendence, and subjective freedom,
Spinoza's system of Nature as one infinite and eternal Substance (*Deus sive Natura*)
embodies the opposite pull: pure immanence, pure objective union, the ego dissolved into
the Greater Order. Wilber presents late-eighteenth-century German Idealism as one long,
serious, and ultimately unsuccessful attempt to "unite Fichte and Spinoza" — to hold
subjective autonomy and objective wholeness together rather than collapsing into either.
Spinoza isn't treated as wrong so much as *half* of an antinomy the culture never
resolved; Wilber notes his continuing pull on "ecomasculinist" and deep-ecology thought
as evidence the Eco pole is still very much alive.

## In *Sex, Ecology, Spirituality*

> [!quote]- "Spinoza uses 'cognition' for knowing an event 'from the inside'"
> Spinoza, Leibniz, Schopenhauer, Whitehead, Aurobindo, Schelling, and Radhakrishnan are
> just a few of the major theorists who have explicitly recognized that the within of
> things, the interiority of individual holons, is in essence the same as
> *consciousness*... Spinoza uses "cognition" for knowing an event "from the inside" and
> "extension" (or matter) for knowing the same event "from the outside."
>
> — *Sex, Ecology, Spirituality*, ch. 4

> [!quote]- "The pantheism of Spinoza (who regarded himself as a good Cartesian)"
> The pantheism of Spinoza (who regarded himself as a good Cartesian), seeing mind and
> matter as two parallel attributes of God that *never* interacted (which he assumed
> took care of *that* problem).
>
> — *Sex, Ecology, Spirituality*, ch. 1

> [!quote]- "'Unite Fichte and Spinoza!'"
> And by the end of the eighteenth century, this tension—between the Ego and the Eco,
> and in its extreme forms, between pure Ascent and pure Descent—this tension had become
> extremely and even unbearably acute, and the watchwords of the age became "unite
> Fichte and Spinoza!"—unite Ego and Eco, unite Ascent and Descent, and thus heal the
> two-thousand-year-old schizoid God that had dominated Western culture virtually from
> its inception.
>
> — *Sex, Ecology, Spirituality*, ch. 13

> [!quote]- "The Eco camps ... all had recourse to Spinoza"
> The Eco camps, on the other hand, all had recourse to Spinoza, suitably interpreted
> for their purposes (this recourse to Spinoza is still quite common in ecomasculinist
> circles)... To the same age, Spinoza stood for Spirit as eternal and total objective
> *Substance* (or infinite and Absolute Object, as Fichte stood for infinite and
> Absolute Subject).
>
> — *Sex, Ecology, Spirituality*, ch. 13

> [!quote]- "'The purpose of Nature is to make men uniform'"
> Spinoza, no doubt, summed it up best: "The purpose of Nature is to make men uniform,
> as children of a common mother."
>
> — Spinoza, quoted in *Sex, Ecology, Spirituality*, ch. 13
